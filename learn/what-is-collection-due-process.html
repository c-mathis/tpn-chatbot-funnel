What Is Collection Due Process?

Collection Due Process (CDP) gives you the right to a hearing before the IRS can levy your property or after they file a tax lien. Understanding this critical right can help you stop aggressive collection actions and explore alternatives.

Collection Due Process (CDP) is a taxpayer protection established by the IRS Restructuring and Reform Act of 1998. It guarantees you the right to a hearing with the IRS Office of Appeals before or after certain collection actions are taken. A CDP hearing is one of the most powerful tools available to taxpayers facing aggressive IRS collection because it can stop levy actions and provides access to Tax Court review.

When Do CDP Rights Apply?

You have CDP rights in two specific situations:

Notice of Intent to Levy

Before the IRS can levy (seize) your wages, bank accounts, or other property, they must send you a Final Notice of Intent to Levy and Notice of Your Right to a Hearing (typically Letter 1058, LT11, or Letter 11). This notice must be sent at least 30 days before any levy action and informs you of your right to request a CDP hearing.

Notice of Federal Tax Lien Filing

After the IRS files a Notice of Federal Tax Lien against your property, they must send you Letter 3172 within five business days. This notice explains your right to request a CDP hearing to challenge the lien or propose alternatives.

Filing Form 12153

To request a CDP hearing, you must file Form 12153, Request for a Collection Due Process or Equivalent Hearing. The form asks for basic information including:

  • Your name, address, and Social Security number
  • The tax periods you want to dispute
  • The type of tax (income, employment, etc.)
  • Why you disagree with the collection action
  • Collection alternatives you want to discuss (installment agreement, Offer in Compromise, etc.)

The Critical 30-Day Deadline

You must submit Form 12153 within 30 days from the date on the CDP notice. This deadline is strictly enforced. If you file after 30 days but within one year, you may still get an "equivalent hearing," but you will lose the right to petition Tax Court if you disagree with the outcome.

What Happens at a CDP Hearing

A CDP hearing is conducted by an Appeals Officer who is independent from the IRS collection division. The hearing is typically conducted by telephone or correspondence, though you can request an in-person meeting. During the hearing, you can:

  • Challenge the collection action: Argue that levy or lien is inappropriate given your circumstances
  • Dispute the tax liability: If you did not receive a statutory notice of deficiency or did not otherwise have an opportunity to dispute the tax
  • Propose alternatives: Request an installment agreement, Offer in Compromise, or currently not collectible status
  • Raise spousal defenses: Claim innocent spouse relief or request separation of liability
  • Challenge procedural errors: Argue that the IRS failed to follow required procedures

CDP Hearing vs. Equivalent Hearing

Understanding the difference between these two types of hearings is crucial:

Feature CDP Hearing Equivalent Hearing
Filing deadline Within 30 days of notice After 30 days, within 1 year
Suspends levy action Yes No
Tax Court review Yes, within 30 days of determination No
Suspends collection statute Yes No

After the CDP Hearing

Following the hearing, the Appeals Officer issues a Notice of Determination explaining their decision. If you agree with the determination, follow the instructions to resolve your case. If you disagree, you have 30 days to file a petition with the U.S. Tax Court.

The ability to petition Tax Court is what makes CDP hearings so valuable. It provides an independent judicial review of your case and creates additional leverage for negotiating with the IRS. However, be aware that the collection statute of limitations is suspended during CDP proceedings and any subsequent court action, which extends the time the IRS has to collect.

Strategic Considerations

CDP hearings can be strategic tools when used appropriately. They can buy time to gather documentation, explore settlement options, or prepare a stronger case. However, they should not be used merely to delay the inevitable, as this can result in additional penalties and interest while extending the collection period.

Given the technical requirements and high stakes involved, working with a qualified tax professional for CDP hearings is strongly recommended. An experienced representative can help you meet all deadlines, present the strongest possible case, and navigate the complex procedural requirements.

Key Points to Remember

30-Day Deadline

You must file Form 12153 within 30 days of the CDP notice to preserve your full rights, including Tax Court review.

Stops Levy Actions

A timely CDP request suspends IRS levy actions during the hearing and any subsequent Tax Court proceedings.

Tax Court Access

CDP hearings preserve your right to petition U.S. Tax Court if you disagree with the Appeals determination.

Propose Alternatives

Use the CDP hearing to propose installment agreements, Offers in Compromise, or currently not collectible status.

Frequently Asked Questions

What triggers a CDP hearing right?

You have the right to request a CDP hearing after receiving a Notice of Intent to Levy (Letter 1058 or LT11) or a Notice of Federal Tax Lien Filing (Letter 3172). These notices specifically inform you of your CDP rights and the deadline to request a hearing.

What is the deadline to request a CDP hearing?

You must request a CDP hearing within 30 days from the date of the notice. If you miss this deadline, you may still be able to request an equivalent hearing, but you will lose your right to petition Tax Court if you disagree with the outcome.

What issues can I raise at a CDP hearing?

At a CDP hearing, you can challenge the appropriateness of collection actions, propose collection alternatives like installment agreements or Offers in Compromise, request spousal defenses, challenge the underlying tax liability (if you did not receive a prior notice of deficiency), and argue that the IRS failed to follow proper procedures.

Does requesting a CDP hearing stop IRS collection?

Yes, requesting a timely CDP hearing generally suspends IRS levy actions during the hearing process and any subsequent Tax Court proceedings. However, the statute of limitations on collection is also suspended during this time, which extends how long the IRS has to collect the debt.

What happens after the CDP hearing?

After the hearing, the Appeals Officer issues a Notice of Determination explaining their decision. If you disagree, you have 30 days to petition the U.S. Tax Court to review the determination. This judicial review is a key advantage of CDP hearings over other administrative appeals.
