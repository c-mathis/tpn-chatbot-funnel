IRS Form 12153 Explained

Your guide to Collection Due Process hearings and how to protect your rights when the IRS takes collection action.

What Is IRS Form 12153?

IRS Form 12153, officially titled "Request for a Collection Due Process or Equivalent Hearing," is a powerful tool that allows taxpayers to challenge IRS collection actions and request an independent review. When the IRS files a tax lien against your property or issues a notice of intent to levy (seize) your assets, you have the right to dispute these actions before an impartial IRS Appeals Officer.

Collection Due Process (CDP) rights were established by Congress to ensure taxpayers receive fair treatment during the collection process. Form 12153 is your formal request to exercise these rights and have your case heard before collection proceeds.

When Can You File Form 12153?

Your right to a CDP hearing is triggered by specific IRS notices:

Notice of Federal Tax Lien Filing

When the IRS files a Notice of Federal Tax Lien (NFTL), you'll receive a notice (such as Letter 3172) informing you of the filing and your CDP rights. A tax lien attaches to all your property and rights to property, including real estate, vehicles, and financial accounts.

Notice of Intent to Levy

Before seizing (levying) your property, the IRS must send you a final notice - typically Letter 1058, LT11, or CP504. This notice informs you that the IRS intends to levy and gives you 30 days to either pay in full or request a hearing.

Notice of Jeopardy Levy

In rare cases where the IRS believes collection is in jeopardy, they may levy before sending the standard notice. In these situations, you still have CDP rights after the levy.

Critical Deadlines

Timing is everything with Form 12153:

30-Day Deadline for CDP Hearing

You must file Form 12153 within 30 days of the date on your CDP notice. This deadline is strictly enforced. Filing a timely request:

  • Suspends IRS collection activity while your hearing is pending
  • Preserves your right to judicial review (Tax Court) if you disagree with the Appeals decision
  • Gives you maximum leverage to negotiate a resolution

One-Year Window for Equivalent Hearing

If you miss the 30-day deadline but file within one year, you can still request an "equivalent hearing." However, this provides fewer protections:

  • Collection activity may continue during the hearing
  • You cannot appeal an unfavorable decision to Tax Court
  • You lose significant negotiating leverage

After One Year

If more than one year has passed since your CDP notice, you cannot request any hearing related to that specific notice. You'll need to explore other resolution options.

What Can You Accomplish in a CDP Hearing?

A CDP hearing provides multiple opportunities to resolve your tax situation:

Challenge the Underlying Tax Liability

If you never had a prior opportunity to dispute the tax amount (such as in an audit or previous appeal), you can raise this issue during your CDP hearing. You can argue that you don't owe the tax, the amount is incorrect, or the IRS made errors in assessment.

Propose Collection Alternatives

You can propose alternatives to full payment:

  • Installment Agreement: A monthly payment plan
  • Offer in Compromise: Settling for less than you owe
  • Currently Not Collectible: Temporary suspension of collection due to hardship
  • Partial Payment Installment Agreement: Payments that won't fully satisfy the debt

Challenge IRS Procedures

You can argue that the IRS failed to follow proper procedures, such as:

  • Not sending required notices
  • Not considering your ability to pay
  • Not following the law in assessing or collecting the tax

Raise Spousal Defenses

If you're being held liable for your spouse's or ex-spouse's tax debt, you can raise innocent spouse relief or request separation of liability during a CDP hearing.

How to Complete Form 12153

Section 1: Taxpayer Information

Enter your name, Social Security Number or EIN, address, and telephone number. Include both day and evening phone numbers where the Appeals Officer can reach you.

Section 2: Notice Information

Provide details from the CDP notice you received:

  • Type of tax (income, employment, etc.)
  • Tax periods involved
  • Date of the notice

Section 3: Hearing Request Type

Check the appropriate box indicating whether you're requesting a CDP hearing (within 30 days) or equivalent hearing (after 30 days but within one year).

Section 4: Basis for Your Request

This is the most important section. Clearly explain why you're requesting the hearing:

  • Check boxes for issues you want to raise (collection alternatives, liability disputes, etc.)
  • Provide specific details about your situation
  • Explain what resolution you're seeking

Section 5: Signature

Sign and date the form. If a representative is handling your case, they can sign if they have a valid Power of Attorney (Form 2848) on file.

The CDP Hearing Process

Here's what to expect after filing:

  1. Acknowledgment: The IRS will acknowledge receipt of your request.
  2. Assignment: Your case is assigned to an Appeals Officer in the IRS Independent Office of Appeals.
  3. Scheduling: You'll be contacted to schedule your hearing. Most hearings are conducted by telephone or correspondence, though you can request an in-person meeting.
  4. Hearing: You present your case, provide documentation, and propose resolutions. The Appeals Officer reviews everything impartially.
  5. Determination: After the hearing, you'll receive a "Notice of Determination" explaining the Appeals Officer's decision.

If You Disagree with the Decision

If you filed a timely CDP request (within 30 days) and disagree with the Notice of Determination, you have 30 days to petition the U.S. Tax Court for judicial review. This is your only opportunity for court review of IRS collection decisions.

If you had an equivalent hearing (filed after 30 days), you cannot appeal to Tax Court. The Notice of Determination is final.

Strategic Considerations

  • Don't miss the 30-day deadline: This is the most important consideration. Mark your calendar and file early.
  • Prepare thoroughly: Gather financial documentation, previous correspondence, and evidence supporting your position.
  • Be specific: Vague requests are less likely to succeed. Clearly state what you want and why.
  • Consider professional help: CDP hearings can be complex. A tax professional can help you navigate the process and present your case effectively.
  • Understand the tradeoff: While a CDP hearing suspends collection, it also suspends the collection statute, potentially giving the IRS more time to collect.

Key Points to Remember

30-Day Deadline Critical

File within 30 days to preserve all your rights, including the ability to appeal to Tax Court if needed.

Stops Collection Activity

A timely CDP request suspends levies, wage garnishments, and most other collection actions while your hearing is pending.

Independent Review

Your case is heard by an Appeals Officer who wasn't involved in the original collection decision - a fresh, impartial look.

Propose Alternatives

Use the hearing to propose installment agreements, offers in compromise, or other collection alternatives.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is IRS Form 12153?

IRS Form 12153 is the Request for a Collection Due Process (CDP) or Equivalent Hearing. It allows taxpayers to request an independent review by the IRS Office of Appeals when the IRS takes collection actions like filing a tax lien or issuing a levy notice. This hearing provides an opportunity to challenge the collection action and propose alternatives.

What triggers the right to file Form 12153?

You can file Form 12153 after receiving specific IRS notices: Notice of Federal Tax Lien Filing (Letter 3172 or similar), Notice of Intent to Levy (Letter 1058, LT11, or similar), or Notice of Jeopardy Levy. These notices inform you of your CDP hearing rights and start the 30-day clock for filing.

What is the deadline to request a CDP hearing?

You must file Form 12153 within 30 days of the date on your CDP notice. This deadline is strictly enforced. Filing after 30 days but within one year results in an equivalent hearing, which provides fewer rights (collection continues and no Tax Court appeal). After one year, you cannot request any hearing for that notice.

What happens during a CDP hearing?

During a CDP hearing, an IRS Appeals Officer independently reviews your case. You can challenge the underlying tax liability (if you haven't had a prior opportunity), propose collection alternatives like payment plans or offers in compromise, argue that the IRS didn't follow proper procedures, or raise spousal defenses.

Does filing Form 12153 stop IRS collection?

Yes, filing a timely CDP hearing request (within 30 days) suspends most IRS collection actions until your hearing is resolved. The IRS cannot levy your wages, bank accounts, or other assets while your CDP case is pending. However, the 10-year collection statute is also suspended during this period.
