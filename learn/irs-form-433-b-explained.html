IRS Form 433-B Explained

A comprehensive guide to the Collection Information Statement for Businesses, including payroll tax debt and business tax resolution options.

What Is IRS Form 433-B?

IRS Form 433-B, officially titled "Collection Information Statement for Businesses," is the financial disclosure form the IRS requires from businesses that owe federal taxes. While Form 433-A handles individual finances, Form 433-B focuses specifically on your business's assets, liabilities, income, and expenses.

This form is essential for business tax resolution because it allows the IRS to evaluate whether your business can pay its tax debt while continuing operations, whether payment terms need to be extended, or whether the business lacks the resources to pay and other solutions are needed.

Who Must File Form 433-B?

Form 433-B is required for business entities with outstanding federal tax liabilities, including:

  • Corporations (C-Corps and S-Corps): Any incorporated business with unpaid corporate income tax, payroll taxes, or excise taxes.
  • Partnerships: Partnerships owing payroll taxes or other business taxes.
  • Limited Liability Companies: LLCs taxed as corporations or partnerships with business tax debt.
  • Sole Proprietors: When business assets are separate from personal assets and the debt relates to business operations.

Note: If you're self-employed and your business and personal finances are intertwined, you may only need Form 433-A. However, if you have employees and owe payroll taxes, Form 433-B is typically required alongside your personal financial statement.

Understanding Business Tax Debt Types

Form 433-B is commonly used for resolving several types of business tax obligations:

Payroll Tax Debt (Form 941 Taxes)

Employment taxes withheld from employee wages, plus the employer's share of Social Security and Medicare taxes. The IRS treats payroll tax debt very seriously because these are "trust fund" taxes - money held in trust for the government. Business owners and responsible persons can be held personally liable through the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty.

Corporate Income Tax

Unpaid federal income taxes owed by C-corporations. Unlike payroll taxes, these don't carry personal liability for shareholders unless fraud is involved.

Excise Taxes

Taxes on specific goods and activities, such as fuel taxes, communications taxes, or environmental taxes applicable to certain industries.

Section-by-Section Guide to Form 433-B

Section 1: Business Information

Provide your business name, EIN (Employer Identification Number), business address, type of entity, and the nature of your business. Include when the business was established and how many employees you have. This section also asks about any related businesses you own or control.

Section 2: Business Personnel

List all partners, officers, major shareholders (those owning 10% or more), and any other principals. Include their titles, ownership percentages, Social Security Numbers, and home addresses. This information helps the IRS determine who may be personally responsible for certain taxes.

Section 3: Business Asset Information

Disclose all business assets with current fair market values:

  • Cash and bank accounts: All business checking, savings, and money market accounts.
  • Accounts receivable: Money owed to your business by customers, with aging information.
  • Inventory: Raw materials, work in progress, and finished goods ready for sale.
  • Equipment and machinery: Tools, computers, manufacturing equipment with values and loan balances.
  • Vehicles: Company cars, trucks, delivery vehicles with values and outstanding loans.
  • Real property: Business-owned real estate, buildings, or land.
  • Investments: Stocks, bonds, or ownership in other businesses.
  • Intangible assets: Patents, trademarks, goodwill, customer lists.

Section 4: Business Liabilities

List all outstanding debts and obligations:

  • Bank loans and lines of credit
  • Accounts payable to vendors and suppliers
  • Mortgages on business property
  • Equipment financing and leases
  • Credit card balances used for business
  • Loans from shareholders or related parties

Section 5: Business Income and Expenses

This section captures your business's monthly cash flow:

Income sources include:

  • Gross receipts from sales or services
  • Rental income from business property
  • Interest and dividend income
  • Other business income

Expenses to report:

  • Materials and inventory purchases
  • Gross wages and salaries (including owner draws)
  • Rent or mortgage payments for business premises
  • Utilities for business locations
  • Vehicle expenses for business use
  • Insurance premiums (liability, property, workers' comp)
  • Professional services (legal, accounting)
  • Taxes (state, local, payroll deposits)
  • Other operating expenses necessary to continue business

Form 433-B (OIC) for Offers in Compromise

When applying for an Offer in Compromise, you'll use Form 433-B (OIC), a modified version designed specifically for OIC applications. Key differences include:

  • Different asset valuation methods focusing on quick sale value
  • Specific multipliers for calculating future business income potential
  • Integration with Form 656 for the overall OIC package
  • More detailed analysis of business viability

Documentation Required

Prepare these documents before completing Form 433-B:

  • Business bank statements (all accounts, 3-6 months)
  • Profit and loss statements (current year and prior year)
  • Balance sheet as of a recent date
  • Accounts receivable aging report
  • Inventory valuation
  • Equipment list with values
  • Vehicle titles and loan statements
  • Real property assessments and mortgage statements
  • Business loan documents
  • Recent business tax returns
  • Payroll records

Special Considerations for Payroll Tax Cases

If your business owes payroll taxes, understand these critical points:

  • Trust Fund Recovery Penalty: The IRS can assess the unpaid trust fund portion (employee withholdings) against responsible individuals personally.
  • Current compliance required: Before approving any payment arrangement, the IRS requires proof that current payroll tax deposits are being made on time.
  • Federal Tax Deposits: You must demonstrate a system to ensure ongoing compliance.
  • Business viability assessment: The IRS evaluates whether keeping your business open serves the government's interest in collecting tax.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Mixing personal and business figures: Keep business finances clearly separated from personal finances.
  • Omitting related party transactions: Disclose loans from shareholders or payments to related businesses.
  • Undervaluing assets: Use realistic fair market values, not book values or wishful thinking.
  • Forgetting accounts receivable: Outstanding customer invoices are assets the IRS can factor into collection potential.
  • Incomplete personnel information: All owners and responsible parties must be listed accurately.

Key Points to Remember

Business-Specific Form

Form 433-B is designed exclusively for business tax situations, separate from your personal finances reported on Form 433-A.

Payroll Taxes Are Serious

Unpaid payroll taxes can result in personal liability for business owners through the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty.

Complete Financial Picture

Report all business assets, liabilities, income, and expenses accurately to determine appropriate payment options.

Current Compliance Required

The IRS requires businesses to be current on all tax filings and deposits before approving payment arrangements.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is IRS Form 433-B?

IRS Form 433-B is the Collection Information Statement for Businesses. It's used by the IRS to evaluate a business's financial situation when the business owes back taxes, including payroll taxes, excise taxes, or corporate income taxes. The form collects comprehensive information about business assets, income, expenses, and ownership to determine what payment arrangements are possible.

Who needs to file Form 433-B?

Any business entity that owes federal taxes and is seeking a payment arrangement with the IRS may need to file Form 433-B. This includes corporations, partnerships, LLCs taxed as businesses, and sole proprietors with significant business tax debt. Self-employed individuals with simple operations typically use Form 433-A instead, but those with employees owing payroll taxes usually need Form 433-B.

What business information is required on Form 433-B?

Form 433-B requires detailed business information including: business bank account balances, accounts receivable, inventory values, equipment and vehicles owned, real property, outstanding loans and lines of credit, gross receipts, monthly operating expenses, and information about owners, partners, and officers. You'll need business bank statements, financial statements, and asset documentation.

Do I need to file Form 433-B along with Form 433-A?

If you're a business owner seeking an Offer in Compromise or installment agreement, you typically need both forms. Form 433-A covers your personal finances, while Form 433-B covers the business. The IRS evaluates both to determine your complete financial picture and overall ability to pay the tax debt.

How does Form 433-B affect payroll tax debt resolution?

For payroll tax debt (Form 941 taxes), Form 433-B is critical because the IRS treats these obligations very seriously. The form helps demonstrate whether the business can pay while remaining operational, or if other solutions should be considered. Owners may face personal liability through the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty, making accurate disclosure essential.
