What Is Back Taxes?

Understanding unpaid taxes from previous years, the consequences of leaving them unresolved, and your options for getting back on track with the IRS.

Back taxes refer to any federal, state, or local taxes that were not paid in the year they were due. This can include income taxes you filed but didn't pay, taxes from returns you never filed, or additional taxes assessed after an audit. Regardless of how they originated, back taxes represent a debt to the government that continues to grow until resolved.

How Back Taxes Accumulate

The moment your tax payment is late, the IRS begins adding penalties and interest to your balance. The failure-to-pay penalty starts at 0.5% of your unpaid taxes per month, up to a maximum of 25%. Interest compounds daily based on the federal short-term rate plus 3%. This means a $10,000 tax debt can grow to over $15,000 in just a few years without any additional taxes being added.

Many taxpayers are surprised to learn their original debt has doubled or tripled by the time they address it. This is why taking action sooner rather than later is so important when dealing with back taxes.

Common Causes of Back Taxes

People end up with back taxes for many different reasons:

  • Self-employment income: Freelancers and contractors often struggle with quarterly estimated tax payments
  • Life changes: Divorce, job loss, medical emergencies, or death of a spouse can make it impossible to keep up with tax obligations
  • Underwithholding: Not having enough taxes withheld from paychecks, especially when working multiple jobs
  • IRS audits: An audit may result in additional taxes owed from previous years
  • Retirement account withdrawals: Early distributions from 401(k)s or IRAs trigger taxes and penalties many don't anticipate
  • Investment gains: Capital gains from selling stocks, real estate, or cryptocurrency can create unexpected tax bills

Consequences of Ignoring Back Taxes

The IRS has significant power to collect unpaid taxes. While they typically start with notices and letters, ignoring back taxes can lead to serious enforcement actions:

  • Federal tax liens: A public notice attached to your property that damages your credit and makes it difficult to sell assets or get loans
  • Wage garnishment: The IRS can take a portion of each paycheck directly from your employer
  • Bank levies: Your bank accounts can be frozen and funds seized
  • Asset seizure: In extreme cases, the IRS can seize and sell your home, vehicles, or other property
  • Passport revocation: If you owe more than $59,000, the State Department can deny or revoke your passport

Resolution Options for Back Taxes

The good news is that the IRS offers several programs to help taxpayers resolve back taxes:

Installment Agreements allow you to pay your debt over time in monthly payments you can afford. For debts under $50,000, you may qualify for a streamlined agreement without extensive financial documentation.

Offer in Compromise lets you settle your tax debt for less than you owe if you can demonstrate you cannot pay the full amount. The IRS accepts these offers when the amount represents the most they can reasonably expect to collect.

Currently Not Collectible Status temporarily pauses IRS collection if paying would cause financial hardship. While interest continues to accrue, you get relief from aggressive collection actions.

Penalty Abatement can remove some or all of the penalties added to your account if you have reasonable cause or qualify for first-time penalty relief.

Taking the First Step

If you have back taxes, the most important thing is to take action. The IRS generally works with taxpayers who proactively try to resolve their tax issues. Start by understanding exactly what you owe, including all years and types of taxes. Then explore which resolution option makes the most sense for your financial situation.

Many taxpayers find that working with a tax professional helps them navigate the process more effectively and often leads to better outcomes than going it alone.

Key Points to Remember

10-Year Collection Limit

The IRS has 10 years from assessment to collect. After that, the debt expires, though certain actions can extend this period.

Debt Grows Rapidly

Penalties up to 25% plus daily compounding interest can double your original tax debt within a few years.

Multiple Resolution Options

IRS programs like installment agreements, Offers in Compromise, and penalty abatement can help resolve back taxes.

Action Prevents Escalation

Taking action before liens and levies are filed protects your credit, wages, and assets from IRS enforcement.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long can the IRS collect back taxes?

The IRS generally has 10 years from the date of assessment to collect back taxes. This is called the Collection Statute Expiration Date (CSED). After this period, the IRS can no longer legally collect the debt. However, certain actions like filing for bankruptcy or submitting an Offer in Compromise can extend this timeframe.

What happens if I don't pay my back taxes?

If you don't pay back taxes, the IRS will add penalties and interest, increasing your debt. They may file a federal tax lien against your property, levy your bank accounts or wages, seize assets, or revoke your passport for debts over $59,000. The longer you wait, the more severe the consequences become.

Can back taxes be forgiven?

Yes, in some cases. The IRS offers programs like Offer in Compromise where you may settle for less than you owe. Penalty abatement can remove certain penalties. Currently Not Collectible status can pause collection if you have financial hardship. In some cases, waiting out the 10-year statute of limitations results in debt expiration.

How do I find out how much I owe in back taxes?

You can find your back tax balance by creating an account on IRS.gov to view your tax account, calling the IRS directly at 1-800-829-1040, or requesting a tax transcript showing your account balance. A tax professional can also pull this information using Form 2848 Power of Attorney.

Will back taxes affect my credit score?

Back taxes themselves don't appear on credit reports. However, if the IRS files a federal tax lien, it becomes public record and can significantly damage your credit score. Liens remain on your credit report for up to 7 years after being released or paid. Resolving tax debt before liens are filed protects your credit.
