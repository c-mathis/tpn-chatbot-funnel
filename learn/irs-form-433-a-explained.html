IRS Form 433-A Explained

A complete guide to the Collection Information Statement for individuals, what to include, and how the IRS uses it to determine your payment options.

What Is IRS Form 433-A?

IRS Form 433-A, officially titled "Collection Information Statement for Wage Earners and Self-Employed Individuals," is a comprehensive financial disclosure document the IRS uses to assess your ability to pay outstanding tax debt. Think of it as a detailed snapshot of your entire financial life.

This form is required when you're seeking tax resolution options that depend on your financial circumstances, including installment agreements, offers in compromise, and currently not collectible (CNC) status. The information you provide directly impacts what payment arrangement the IRS will approve.

When Is Form 433-A Required?

You'll typically need to complete Form 433-A in several situations:

  • Installment agreements over $50,000: For larger payment plans, the IRS needs detailed financial information to structure appropriate payments.
  • Offer in Compromise applications: The OIC version (Form 433-A OIC) calculates your reasonable collection potential.
  • Currently Not Collectible status: To prove you cannot afford any payment, you must demonstrate financial hardship through this form.
  • IRS collection investigations: Revenue officers may request this form during active collection efforts.
  • Partial payment installment agreements: When you can't pay your full balance before the statute expires.

Section-by-Section Guide to Form 433-A

Section 1: Personal Information

Provide your full legal name, address, Social Security Number, date of birth, and marital status. If married, your spouse's information is also required even if filing separately. Include details about dependents you support, as this affects allowable expense calculations.

Section 2: Employment Information

List your current employer's name, address, and how long you've worked there. For self-employed individuals, describe your business, how long you've operated, and the average number of employees. This section also asks about previous employers if you've changed jobs recently.

Section 3: Personal Asset Information

This is where you disclose everything you own:

  • Cash and bank accounts: Checking, savings, money market accounts at all financial institutions.
  • Investments: Stocks, bonds, mutual funds, certificates of deposit.
  • Retirement accounts: 401(k), IRA, pension plans, SEP accounts.
  • Life insurance: Cash value of whole life policies.
  • Real property: Your home, rental properties, land, with current market values and loan balances.
  • Vehicles: Cars, trucks, motorcycles, boats, RVs with values and outstanding loans.
  • Other assets: Artwork, jewelry, collectibles, business equipment.

Section 4: Credit Card and Credit Information

List all credit cards with their balances and available credit. While credit card payments aren't typically considered allowable expenses, this information helps the IRS understand your complete financial picture and whether you have access to credit that could pay the tax debt.

Section 5: Income Information

Report all sources of income for you and your spouse:

  • Wages, salaries, and tips (use gross amounts)
  • Self-employment income
  • Social Security benefits
  • Pension and retirement distributions
  • Rental income
  • Interest and dividends
  • Alimony and child support received
  • Any other income sources

Section 6: Monthly Living Expenses

This crucial section determines your disposable income. The IRS compares your expenses against national and local standards. Typical categories include:

  • Food, clothing, and miscellaneous: Based on IRS national standards by household size.
  • Housing and utilities: Mortgage/rent, property taxes, insurance, gas, electric, water, phone.
  • Transportation: Car payments, insurance, fuel, public transportation, parking.
  • Health care: Insurance premiums, out-of-pocket medical expenses, prescriptions.
  • Court-ordered payments: Child support, alimony obligations.
  • Other necessary expenses: Childcare, student loans, union dues.

Understanding IRS Collection Financial Standards

The IRS doesn't simply accept whatever expenses you report. They apply standardized expense allowances:

National Standards: These are fixed amounts for food, clothing, housekeeping supplies, personal care products, and miscellaneous expenses. The amount depends on your household size and income level. You generally cannot exceed these amounts.

Local Standards: Housing and transportation allowances vary by geographic area. The IRS publishes county-specific limits for housing (including utilities) and regional limits for vehicle ownership and operating costs.

Necessary Expenses: Some expenses like health care and court-ordered payments are allowed at actual cost if reasonable. The IRS may question unusually high amounts.

Documentation You'll Need

Gather these documents before starting Form 433-A:

  • Pay stubs for the past 3 months
  • Bank statements for all accounts (3 months)
  • Investment and retirement account statements
  • Recent mortgage statement or lease agreement
  • Vehicle registrations and loan statements
  • Utility bills (one recent bill for each utility)
  • Health insurance premium statements
  • Property tax bills and homeowner's insurance
  • Court orders for child support or alimony
  • Self-employment profit and loss statements

Form 433-A vs. Form 433-A (OIC)

If you're applying for an Offer in Compromise, you'll use Form 433-A (OIC) instead of the standard 433-A. The OIC version has some key differences:

  • Different expense calculations specific to OIC eligibility
  • Includes multipliers for calculating future income potential
  • Focuses on determining your "reasonable collection potential"
  • Must be submitted with Form 656 and supporting documents

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Underreporting assets: The IRS can verify your assets through various databases. Omissions damage your credibility.
  • Overstating expenses: Only claim expenses you can document. Inflated figures will be rejected.
  • Missing bank accounts: Include all accounts, even those with small balances.
  • Forgetting retirement accounts: These count as assets even if there are penalties for early withdrawal.
  • Using net income instead of gross: Always report gross income before deductions.
  • Outdated information: The form must reflect your current situation, not circumstances from months ago.

Key Points to Remember

Complete Disclosure

Report all assets and income honestly. The IRS can verify information through third-party sources and penalize false statements.

IRS Standards Apply

Your claimed expenses are compared against IRS national and local standards. Amounts above these limits typically aren't allowed.

Documentation Required

Back up every figure with supporting documents. Bank statements, pay stubs, and bills prove your financial situation.

Determines Your Options

Your Form 433-A directly impacts what payment plans you qualify for and how much you'll need to pay monthly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is IRS Form 433-A?

IRS Form 433-A is the Collection Information Statement for Wage Earners and Self-Employed Individuals. It's a detailed financial disclosure form used by the IRS to evaluate your ability to pay tax debt. This form is required for installment agreements over $50,000, offers in compromise, and currently not collectible status requests.

What information do I need for Form 433-A?

You'll need comprehensive financial documents including: pay stubs for the last 3 months, bank statements for all accounts, investment and retirement account statements, vehicle registrations and loan documents, mortgage statements, utility bills, and documentation of all monthly living expenses. Having everything organized before you start will make the process much smoother.

What is the difference between Form 433-A and Form 433-A (OIC)?

Form 433-A is used for general collection purposes like installment agreements and CNC status. Form 433-A (OIC) is a modified version specifically designed for Offer in Compromise applications, with different calculations for determining your reasonable collection potential. The OIC version includes multipliers for future income and uses different expense allowance rules.

How does the IRS use Form 433-A to calculate payments?

The IRS uses Form 433-A to calculate your disposable income by subtracting allowable living expenses from your gross monthly income. This determines what you can afford to pay each month. The IRS also reviews your assets to determine if you have equity that could be liquidated to pay your debt more quickly.

What expenses are allowable on Form 433-A?

The IRS allows necessary living expenses based on national and local standards, including housing, utilities, food, clothing, transportation, health care, and court-ordered payments like child support. Luxury expenses like cable TV, private school tuition, credit card minimum payments, or charitable contributions are generally not allowed as deductions from your income.
