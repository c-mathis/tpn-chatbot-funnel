What Is an IRS Audit?

An IRS audit is a review of your tax return to verify that income and deductions are reported accurately. Understanding the different types of audits and your rights can help you navigate the process with confidence.

An IRS audit is an examination of your tax return and financial records to ensure you have reported your income, deductions, and credits accurately. While the word "audit" can cause anxiety, understanding the process and your rights can help you respond appropriately and protect your interests.

The Three Types of IRS Audits

The IRS conducts three main types of audits, each varying in complexity and how they are conducted:

1. Correspondence Audit

The most common and least invasive type, a correspondence audit is conducted entirely by mail. The IRS sends you a letter identifying specific items on your return that need verification or clarification. You respond by mailing the requested documents, and the IRS reviews them without an in-person meeting. These audits typically focus on one or two specific issues, such as verifying a deduction or credit.

2. Office Audit

An office audit requires you to visit an IRS office to meet with an auditor in person. These audits are more comprehensive than correspondence audits and may cover multiple areas of your return. You will receive a letter specifying what documents to bring and which items are being examined. Office audits often focus on income verification, itemized deductions, and business expenses.

3. Field Audit

The most thorough type of audit, a field audit involves an IRS agent visiting your home, business, or accountant's office. These audits typically target higher-income individuals, business owners, and complex returns. Field audits can take weeks or months and may examine your entire tax return, including lifestyle factors and business operations. Because of their scope, having professional representation during a field audit is strongly recommended.

Common Audit Triggers

While IRS selection methods are not entirely transparent, certain factors are known to increase audit risk:

  • Unreported income: The IRS receives copies of your W-2s, 1099s, and other income documents. Discrepancies between reported income and these forms often trigger audits.
  • High deductions relative to income: Claiming deductions that seem unusually large compared to your income level can raise red flags.
  • Self-employment income: Self-employed individuals face higher audit rates due to the potential for unreported income and overstated expenses.
  • Large charitable contributions: Claiming charitable donations significantly higher than average for your income bracket may trigger scrutiny.
  • Home office deductions: While legitimate, this deduction is often claimed incorrectly, making it an audit target.
  • Rental losses: Claiming significant losses from rental properties, especially if you have other income, can attract attention.
  • Math errors: Simple mistakes can lead to correspondence audits to verify the correct figures.

Your Rights During an Audit

The IRS Taxpayer Bill of Rights guarantees you specific protections during an audit:

  • Right to representation: You can have a tax professional (enrolled agent, CPA, or tax attorney) represent you at any time during the audit.
  • Right to know why: The IRS must explain why they are asking for information and how it will be used.
  • Right to appeal: If you disagree with audit findings, you can appeal the decision through the IRS Appeals process.
  • Right to confidentiality: Your tax information is protected and cannot be disclosed without authorization.
  • Right to finality: You have the right to know the maximum time you have to challenge the IRS's position and the maximum time the IRS has to audit a particular tax year.

What to Do If You Receive an Audit Notice

If you receive an audit notice, do not panic. Follow these steps:

  1. Read the notice carefully: Understand exactly what the IRS is requesting and the deadline for response.
  2. Gather documentation: Collect all records related to the items being questioned, including receipts, bank statements, and supporting documents.
  3. Consider professional help: For anything beyond a simple correspondence audit, having a qualified tax professional represent you can significantly improve outcomes.
  4. Respond on time: Meet all deadlines. Request extensions in writing if needed.
  5. Only provide what is requested: Do not volunteer additional information beyond what the IRS specifically asks for.

Remember, an audit does not automatically mean you owe additional taxes. Many audits result in no change or even a refund if the IRS finds you overpaid.

Key Points to Remember

Know Your Rights

You have the right to representation, to know why information is requested, and to appeal any findings you disagree with.

Keep Good Records

Maintain organized records for at least 3-7 years. Good documentation is your best defense in any audit situation.

Respond Promptly

Always respond to IRS notices by the deadline. Ignoring an audit notice makes the situation significantly worse.

Get Professional Help

For office and field audits, professional representation significantly improves outcomes and reduces stress.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the chances of being audited by the IRS?

The IRS audits less than 1% of all individual tax returns. However, your audit risk increases with higher income levels, self-employment, large deductions relative to income, and certain red flags like unreported income or math errors.

How far back can the IRS audit my tax returns?

Generally, the IRS can audit returns filed within the last 3 years. However, this extends to 6 years if you omitted more than 25% of gross income, and there is no limit if you filed a fraudulent return or failed to file at all.

What happens if I ignore an IRS audit notice?

Ignoring an IRS audit notice is a serious mistake. The IRS will proceed without your input and make assessments based on available information, which typically results in the maximum tax, penalties, and interest. You lose your ability to dispute their findings if you do not respond.

Can I have someone represent me during an IRS audit?

Yes, you have the right to be represented by a qualified tax professional during an audit. Enrolled agents, CPAs, and tax attorneys can represent you before the IRS with a valid Power of Attorney (Form 2848). They can communicate with the IRS on your behalf and even attend meetings without you present.

What triggers an IRS audit?

Common audit triggers include mathematical errors, unreported income (the IRS receives copies of your W-2s and 1099s), unusually high deductions relative to income, home office deductions, large charitable contributions, business losses year after year, and foreign bank accounts. The IRS also uses computer algorithms to flag returns that deviate from statistical norms.
