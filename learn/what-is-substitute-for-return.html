What Is Substitute for Return?

Understanding what happens when the IRS files a tax return on your behalf, why it usually means a bigger tax bill, and how to respond.

A Substitute for Return (SFR) is a tax return prepared by the IRS on your behalf when you fail to file. Under Section 6020(b) of the Internal Revenue Code, the IRS has the authority to prepare returns for non-filers using income information they already have. While this might sound convenient, an SFR almost always results in a higher tax bill than if you had filed yourself.

How the IRS Creates an SFR

The IRS receives copies of all the income documents sent to you throughout the year:

  • W-2s from employers showing wages and withholding
  • 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC for contractor or freelance income
  • 1099-INT and 1099-DIV for interest and dividends
  • 1099-B for stock sales and investment transactions
  • 1099-K for payment card and third-party network transactions
  • 1099-R for retirement distributions
  • 1099-G for unemployment and state tax refunds

When you don't file, the IRS uses this information to construct a return. However, they only know about income that was reported to them. They don't know about your deductions, credits, expenses, or personal circumstances.

Why SFRs Result in Higher Taxes

An SFR is essentially a worst-case calculation of your tax liability:

Unfavorable filing status: The IRS uses "Single" or "Married Filing Separately" status with only one personal exemption, even if you could claim "Head of Household" or "Married Filing Jointly" with multiple exemptions.

No deductions: The IRS applies only the standard deduction. They don't know about itemized deductions like mortgage interest, charitable contributions, or state taxes paid.

No business expenses: If you're self-employed, the IRS shows all 1099 income as taxable without any deductions for business expenses, home office, vehicle use, or other costs.

No credits: Credits like the Earned Income Tax Credit, Child Tax Credit, education credits, and retirement savings credits are not applied.

No cost basis: For investment sales, the IRS may assume zero cost basis, making the entire proceeds taxable as gain.

No loss carryovers: Capital losses, business losses, or other carryforward amounts are not considered.

The SFR Process Timeline

Here's how the SFR process typically unfolds:

  1. Initial notices: The IRS sends reminders that you haven't filed (CP59, CP515, CP518)
  2. SFR preparation: If you don't respond, the IRS prepares a Substitute for Return
  3. Notice of Deficiency: You receive a CP3219A giving you 90 days to file your own return or petition Tax Court
  4. Assessment: If you don't respond within 90 days, the IRS assesses the tax and it becomes legally owed
  5. Collection begins: The IRS starts sending balance due notices and may proceed to liens and levies

The entire process from first notice to assessment can take 1-2 years, but once assessment occurs, collection can begin immediately.

How to Respond to an SFR

If the IRS has filed an SFR, you have options:

Before assessment (within 90-day window): File your own return as quickly as possible. If you respond within the 90-day period after receiving the Notice of Deficiency, your return will be processed instead of the SFR.

After assessment: You can still file your own return. You'll need to request "Audit Reconsideration" to have the IRS review your return and adjust your account. This process takes longer but can still significantly reduce what you owe.

Gather your records: Collect W-2s, 1099s, receipts for deductions, and any other documentation. If you're missing documents, you can request wage and income transcripts from the IRS.

Consider professional help: If you have multiple years of SFRs or complex tax situations, working with a tax professional can ensure your returns are prepared correctly and you claim all available deductions and credits.

Replacing an SFR Often Reduces Your Debt

Many taxpayers are relieved to discover that filing their own return dramatically reduces what they owe. Common scenarios include:

  • Self-employed with expenses: A $50,000 SFR bill might become $15,000 when business expenses are properly deducted
  • Parents with children: Missing Child Tax Credit and dependent exemptions can mean thousands in overstated tax
  • Homeowners: Mortgage interest and property tax deductions often make a significant difference
  • Low-income earners: The Earned Income Tax Credit can turn a tax bill into a refund

Even after assessment, replacing an SFR is almost always worthwhile if your actual tax liability is lower.

SFRs and Your Tax Relief Options

Having SFRs on your record affects your tax relief options:

Filing requirement: You must replace SFRs with your own returns before you can qualify for an Offer in Compromise, Installment Agreement, or most other IRS programs.

Accurate debt calculation: You can't negotiate a settlement until you know your actual tax liability. SFR amounts are almost never accurate.

Penalty relief: While you'll still face failure-to-file and failure-to-pay penalties, reasonable cause abatement may apply depending on your circumstances.

The first step in resolving unfiled tax returns is always to file accurate returns, whether or not the IRS has already created SFRs.

Preventing Future SFRs

The best approach is to file every year, even if you can't pay:

  • Filing without paying is far better than not filing at all
  • The failure-to-file penalty (5% per month) is much higher than failure-to-pay (0.5% per month)
  • Filing protects your right to deductions and credits
  • You maintain control over your tax situation

If you're behind on multiple years, getting caught up may feel overwhelming. Breaking it down year by year, starting with the oldest, makes the process manageable. Professional help can make it faster and ensure accuracy.

Key Points to Remember

Higher Tax Bills

SFRs use worst-case assumptions: single status, no deductions, no credits, no expenses.

You Can Replace It

File your own return at any time to replace an SFR with accurate information and lower your tax bill.

90-Day Window

After receiving a Notice of Deficiency, you have 90 days to file your own return or petition Tax Court.

Required for Relief

You must replace SFRs with actual returns before qualifying for most IRS resolution programs.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Substitute for Return?

A Substitute for Return (SFR) is a tax return the IRS prepares on your behalf when you fail to file. The IRS uses income information reported by your employers and financial institutions (W-2s, 1099s) to calculate what you owe. However, SFRs typically result in higher tax bills because the IRS uses unfavorable filing status, doesn't include deductions or credits you may be entitled to, and assumes all reported income is taxable.

Why does an SFR usually result in higher taxes?

SFRs result in higher taxes because the IRS uses single filing status with only one exemption (even if you're married or have dependents), doesn't include itemized deductions, business expenses, or adjustments, doesn't apply tax credits like Earned Income Credit or Child Tax Credit, and may include income that could be offset by losses or expenses. Essentially, the IRS calculates your worst-case tax scenario.

Can I replace an SFR with my own return?

Yes, you can file your own return to replace an SFR at any time. Your return should include all proper deductions, credits, and correct filing status. If your return shows lower tax than the SFR, the IRS will adjust your account. However, you'll still owe any legitimate tax plus penalties and interest from the original due date. Filing a correct return often significantly reduces what you owe.

What happens after the IRS files an SFR?

After filing an SFR, the IRS assesses the calculated tax against you and sends a Notice of Deficiency (CP3219A) giving you 90 days to respond. If you don't respond, the assessment becomes final and the IRS begins collection actions including sending balance due notices, filing tax liens, levying wages and bank accounts, and intercepting tax refunds. You can still file your own return after assessment, but you may need to go through audit reconsideration to adjust your account.

How do I know if the IRS filed an SFR for me?

You'll receive IRS notices indicating an SFR was filed. Common notices include CP59 (reminder that you didn't file), CP515/CP518 (additional filing reminders), and CP3219A (Notice of Deficiency based on SFR). You can also request your IRS account transcript, which will show "TC 150" with a code indicating it's an SFR rather than your filed return. If you've been ignoring IRS mail, you may have SFRs you don't know about.
