What Is the IRS Statute of Limitations?

The IRS has a limited time to collect tax debts. Understanding the Collection Statute Expiration Date (CSED) and the events that extend it can help you make informed decisions about resolving your tax situation.

The IRS statute of limitations on collection refers to the legally mandated time period during which the IRS can collect a tax debt. Once this period expires, the IRS must stop collection efforts and write off the remaining balance. This deadline is known as the Collection Statute Expiration Date, or CSED.

The 10-Year Collection Rule

Under Internal Revenue Code Section 6502, the IRS generally has 10 years from the date of assessment to collect a tax debt. The assessment date is when the IRS officially records your tax liability on its books, which typically occurs:

  • Shortly after you file a return with a balance due
  • After an audit results in additional tax owed
  • When the IRS files a substitute return for unfiled tax years
  • After certain penalties are assessed

Each tax year and each type of assessment has its own CSED. This means you may have multiple CSEDs if you owe taxes for several years or have been assessed additional amounts through audits or penalties.

Tolling Events That Extend the CSED

Certain events "toll" or pause the 10-year clock. When the statute is tolled, the collection period is extended by the length of the tolling event plus additional time in some cases. Common tolling events include:

Offer in Compromise

Submitting an Offer in Compromise suspends the collection statute while the offer is pending, plus an additional 30 days after rejection or withdrawal. If you appeal a rejected offer, the statute remains suspended during the appeal process.

Installment Agreement Request

Requesting an installment agreement tolls the statute while the request is pending. If the IRS rejects the request and you appeal, the statute remains suspended during the appeal period.

Collection Due Process Hearing

Requesting a CDP hearing suspends the collection statute during the hearing and any subsequent Tax Court litigation.

Bankruptcy

Filing for bankruptcy automatically suspends IRS collection activity and the statute of limitations. The statute is tolled during the bankruptcy plus 6 months after discharge or dismissal.

Living Outside the United States

If you live outside the U.S. continuously for more than 6 months, the collection statute may be suspended for the time you are abroad.

Military Service

Active duty military members may have the statute suspended under the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act.

How to Determine Your CSED

Finding your exact CSED can be challenging because you need to know:

  1. The original assessment date: This is when the tax was officially recorded on the IRS books
  2. Any tolling events: Every event that suspended the statute must be accounted for
  3. Extensions you may have signed: Older installment agreements sometimes included statute extensions

You can request your IRS account transcript to see assessment dates, but calculating tolling events requires careful analysis. A tax professional with access to the IRS Practitioner Priority Service can request CSED information directly from the IRS.

What Happens When the CSED Expires

When the collection statute expires, the IRS must:

  • Stop all collection actions on that debt
  • Release any federal tax liens related to that debt
  • Write off the remaining balance as uncollectible

The debt essentially disappears from a legal standpoint. However, you cannot claim a refund for payments made before the expiration, and the IRS may offset any future refunds against other tax years that are still within the collection period.

Strategic Considerations

Understanding the CSED is valuable for tax planning, but "running out the clock" is rarely a practical strategy. Consider these factors:

  • Active collection: The IRS will aggressively pursue collection during the 10 years, including liens, levies, and garnishments
  • Interest and penalties: Your balance continues to grow with interest and penalties
  • Credit damage: Tax liens damage your credit and can make it difficult to buy a home, get a car loan, or even rent an apartment
  • Tolling events: Many resolution options extend the statute, potentially adding years to the collection period

For most taxpayers, resolving the debt through an Offer in Compromise, installment agreement, or currently not collectible status provides a better path forward than waiting for the statute to expire.

Special Situations

Amended Returns and Audits

If you file an amended return or the IRS audits you and assesses additional tax, each new assessment has its own 10-year collection period starting from the assessment date.

Judgments

If the IRS reduces your tax debt to a civil judgment, the judgment may have a longer collection period depending on state law and federal rules.

Trust Fund Recovery Penalty

The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty has its own 10-year CSED starting from the date it was assessed, which is separate from the underlying business tax liability.

Key Points to Remember

10-Year Limit

The IRS has 10 years from the assessment date to collect a tax debt. After the CSED, the balance is written off.

Tolling Events

OICs, installment agreements, CDP hearings, and bankruptcy all pause the 10-year clock and extend the CSED.

Check Your Transcripts

IRS account transcripts show assessment dates. A tax professional can help calculate your exact CSED.

Active Collection Continues

Do not wait out the statute. The IRS will pursue liens, levies, and garnishments during the 10-year period.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does the IRS have to collect tax debt?

The IRS generally has 10 years from the date of assessment to collect a tax debt. This is called the Collection Statute Expiration Date (CSED). After the CSED passes, the IRS can no longer legally collect the debt, and any remaining balance is written off.

What events extend the IRS collection statute?

Several events toll (pause) the 10-year collection period, including: submitting an Offer in Compromise, requesting an installment agreement, filing bankruptcy, requesting a Collection Due Process hearing, living outside the U.S. for more than 6 months, and certain military service deferrals. The statute resumes after these events conclude.

How can I find out my CSED?

You can find your Collection Statute Expiration Date by requesting your IRS account transcripts, which may show assessment dates. However, calculating the actual CSED requires accounting for any tolling events. A tax professional with IRS Practitioner Priority Service access can obtain CSED information directly from the IRS.

Does the statute of limitations apply to all tax debts?

The 10-year collection statute applies to most federal tax assessments. However, if you signed an agreement extending the statute (common in installment agreements before 2000), agreed to extend it as part of an audit, or have tax debt that was reduced to a judgment, different rules may apply.

Should I wait out the statute of limitations instead of settling?

Waiting out the CSED is rarely a good strategy. During the collection period, the IRS can file liens, levy bank accounts, garnish wages, and seize assets. Interest and penalties continue accruing. A tax lien damages your credit for years. For most people, resolving the debt through an installment agreement, Offer in Compromise, or other settlement is a better approach.
