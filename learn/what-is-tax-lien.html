What Is a Tax Lien?

A federal tax lien is the government's legal claim against your property when you fail to pay your tax debt. It protects the IRS's interest in all your assets.

When you owe back taxes and don't pay after the IRS sends a bill, the government can file a Notice of Federal Tax Lien. This lien is a public record that alerts creditors that the government has a legal right to your property. Understanding how tax liens work is crucial for protecting your financial interests and finding a path to resolution.

How Does a Tax Lien Arise?

A federal tax lien comes into existence automatically when three conditions are met:

  1. Assessment: The IRS assesses a tax liability against you
  2. Notice and demand: The IRS sends you a bill explaining how much you owe
  3. Failure to pay: You don't pay the full amount within 10 days of the notice

At this point, a statutory lien exists. However, for the lien to be effective against other creditors and purchasers, the IRS must file a Notice of Federal Tax Lien (NFTL) with your state or county recorder's office. This makes the lien a matter of public record.

What Does a Tax Lien Attach To?

A federal tax lien is comprehensive. It attaches to:

  • Real property: Your home, land, rental properties
  • Personal property: Vehicles, jewelry, furniture, equipment
  • Financial assets: Bank accounts, investment accounts, retirement accounts
  • Business assets: Inventory, accounts receivable, business equipment
  • Future property: Assets you acquire after the lien is filed

Tax Lien vs. Tax Levy: Understanding the Difference

Many people confuse tax liens and tax levies, but they're different:

Tax Lien

A lien is a claim against your property. It doesn't take anything from you directly but secures the government's interest. Think of it as the IRS putting a "hold" on your assets. You still own and can use your property, but the lien affects your ability to sell or borrow against it.

Tax Levy

A levy is the actual seizure of your property to satisfy the tax debt. The IRS can levy your bank accounts, garnish your wages, or seize and sell your property. A levy is an enforcement action, while a lien is a protective measure.

How Tax Liens Affect You

Impact on Credit and Financing

While tax liens were removed from credit reports in 2018, they can still affect you:

  • Lenders often search public records and will find the lien
  • Mortgage applications typically require lien resolution
  • Business financing becomes more difficult
  • The lien appears on title searches when selling property

Impact on Selling Property

If you try to sell property with a tax lien:

  • The lien must typically be satisfied from sale proceeds
  • Title companies won't close without addressing the lien
  • If proceeds are insufficient, you may need IRS cooperation

Impact on Bankruptcy

Federal tax liens survive bankruptcy in many cases. Even if the underlying tax debt is discharged, the lien may remain attached to property you owned at the time of filing.

How to Remove or Address a Tax Lien

1. Pay Your Tax Debt in Full

The most straightforward solution. Once paid, the IRS will release the lien within 30 days. You can also request a Certificate of Release.

2. Lien Discharge

A discharge removes the lien from specific property. This is useful when selling property and the proceeds will be applied to your debt. Request using Form 14135.

3. Lien Subordination

Subordination doesn't remove the lien but allows other creditors to move ahead of the IRS lien. This can help you refinance or obtain a loan. Request using Form 14134.

4. Lien Withdrawal

A withdrawal removes the public Notice of Federal Tax Lien while the lien itself may still exist. This can be granted if:

  • You enter a direct debit installment agreement (for debts under $25,000)
  • The lien was filed prematurely or not according to IRS procedures
  • Withdrawal will help you pay the tax debt faster

Request withdrawal using Form 12277 after the lien is released.

Key Points to Remember

Claim, Not Seizure

A lien is the IRS's legal claim to your property. It doesn't take your assets but affects your ability to sell or borrow.

$25,000 Threshold

Under Fresh Start, the IRS generally won't file liens for debts under $25,000 if you're making payments.

Public Record

Once filed, the Notice of Federal Tax Lien becomes public record and can be found by anyone searching.

Removal Options

Liens can be released, discharged, subordinated, or withdrawn depending on your situation and needs.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a tax lien and a tax levy?

A tax lien is a legal claim against your property that secures the government's interest in your assets. It doesn't take your property but affects your ability to sell or borrow against it. A tax levy is the actual seizure of your property - taking money from bank accounts, garnishing wages, or seizing and selling assets. A lien protects the IRS's interest; a levy enforces collection.

How does a tax lien affect my credit score?

While federal tax liens are no longer included on credit reports from the three major bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) as of 2018, they can still affect you. The lien is public record and can be found by lenders, landlords, and others who search public records. It can make obtaining loans, mortgages, or lines of credit difficult and may affect background checks.

Can I sell my house if there's a tax lien on it?

Yes, but the tax lien must be satisfied from the sale proceeds before you receive any money. The IRS lien attaches to all your property, including real estate. When you sell, the title company will require the lien be paid from proceeds. If your home has insufficient equity, you may need to negotiate with the IRS to release the lien or subordinate it to allow the sale.

How do I get a tax lien removed?

There are several ways to remove a tax lien: 1) Pay your tax debt in full - the lien will be released within 30 days, 2) Enter a direct debit installment agreement for under $25,000 and request lien withdrawal, 3) Request a discharge of specific property when selling, 4) Request subordination to allow refinancing. You may also request lien withdrawal after it's released using Form 12277.

When does the IRS file a tax lien?

The IRS typically files a Notice of Federal Tax Lien after you've been assessed a tax, received a bill, and failed to pay within 10 days. Under Fresh Start guidelines, liens generally aren't filed for debts under $25,000. The IRS has discretion and may delay filing if you enter into a payment agreement quickly. Once filed, the lien becomes public record.
