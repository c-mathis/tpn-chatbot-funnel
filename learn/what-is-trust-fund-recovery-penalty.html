What Is the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty?

The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty (TFRP) makes individuals personally liable for unpaid payroll taxes. Understanding who is at risk and how to respond is critical for business owners, officers, and anyone involved in payroll decisions.

The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty is one of the most aggressive tools in the IRS's collection arsenal. When a business fails to pay over employment taxes withheld from employee wages, the IRS can assess a penalty equal to 100% of the unpaid trust fund taxes against individuals who were responsible for collecting and paying these taxes. This penalty allows the IRS to pursue collection from personal assets even when the business has no resources or has closed.

What Are Trust Fund Taxes?

When you have employees, you are required to withhold certain taxes from their paychecks and pay them to the IRS. The "trust fund" portion includes:

  • Federal income tax withholding: Based on the employee's W-4 elections
  • Employee's share of Social Security tax: 6.2% of wages up to the annual limit
  • Employee's share of Medicare tax: 1.45% of all wages (plus 0.9% additional Medicare tax on high earners)

These are called "trust fund" taxes because the employer holds them in trust on behalf of the government. The money never belonged to the employer; it is the employees' contribution toward their tax obligations.

Not included in trust fund taxes:

  • Employer's share of Social Security and Medicare taxes
  • Federal Unemployment Tax (FUTA)
  • Interest and penalties assessed against the business

Who Is a "Responsible Person"?

The TFRP can be assessed against anyone who meets two criteria:

1. Responsibility

You had the duty or authority to collect, account for, and pay over the trust fund taxes. This can include:

  • Authority to sign checks or direct payments
  • Control over the business's financial affairs
  • Decision-making power over which creditors to pay
  • Authority to hire and fire employees

Common responsible persons include:

  • Business owners and partners
  • Corporate officers (CEO, CFO, President)
  • Directors with operational involvement
  • Shareholders who participate in business decisions
  • Bookkeepers and payroll managers with check-signing authority
  • Outside accountants or service providers with payment authority

2. Willfulness

You willfully failed to collect or pay over the taxes. Willfulness does not require intent to defraud; it means you knew the taxes were due and either intentionally disregarded the obligation or were recklessly indifferent to whether the taxes were paid.

Common examples of willful conduct:

  • Paying other creditors (vendors, rent, loans) instead of the IRS
  • Continuing to operate the business knowing taxes were unpaid
  • Ignoring warnings from accountants or payroll services
  • Using withheld taxes to keep the business afloat

The TFRP Investigation Process

When a business fails to pay employment taxes, the IRS assigns a Revenue Officer to investigate. The process typically includes:

Step 1: Form 4180 Interview

The Revenue Officer interviews anyone who may be a responsible person. You are not required to participate in this interview, but declining may result in the IRS making determinations based on other evidence. Having a tax professional present during the interview is strongly recommended.

Step 2: Letter 1153 (60-Day Letter)

If the IRS determines you are a responsible person, they send Letter 1153 proposing to assess the TFRP against you personally. You have 60 days to appeal this determination before it becomes final.

Step 3: Assessment

If you do not respond or your appeal is unsuccessful, the IRS assesses the TFRP. It then appears on your personal tax account and the IRS can begin collection actions against your personal assets.

How Much Is the TFRP?

The TFRP equals 100% of the unpaid trust fund taxes. Despite being called a "penalty," it is essentially a mechanism to collect the same taxes from responsible individuals that the business failed to pay.

Example:

Federal Income Tax Withheld $15,000
Employee Social Security (6.2%) $6,200
Employee Medicare (1.45%) $1,450
Trust Fund Recovery Penalty $22,650

Resolving TFRP Assessments

If you have been assessed the TFRP, several resolution options may be available:

  • Installment agreement: Make monthly payments toward the TFRP
  • Offer in Compromise: Settle for less than the full amount if you qualify
  • Currently not collectible: Temporarily suspend collection if you cannot afford payments
  • Litigation: Pay a small amount and sue for refund in district court

The TFRP has its own 10-year collection statute, separate from the business liability. This means careful analysis of your situation is essential for developing the right resolution strategy.

Protecting Yourself

If you are involved in a business with payroll, take steps to protect yourself:

  • Monitor payroll tax deposits regularly
  • Document any objections to non-payment in writing
  • Resign from positions if owners refuse to pay trust fund taxes
  • Consult a tax professional immediately if you learn taxes are unpaid

The TFRP is a serious matter that can follow you for years and affect your personal finances, credit, and ability to get loans. If you have received any notice related to unpaid payroll taxes, seek professional assistance immediately.

Key Points to Remember

100% Personal Liability

The TFRP equals the full amount of unpaid trust fund taxes and is assessed against you personally.

Multiple Persons Liable

The IRS can assess multiple people for the same TFRP. Anyone with authority over payments may be targeted.

60-Day Appeal Window

You have 60 days from Letter 1153 to appeal before the TFRP is assessed. Do not ignore this notice.

Separate Statute

The TFRP has its own 10-year collection period, separate from the business tax liability.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are trust fund taxes?

Trust fund taxes are the federal income tax and employee share of Social Security and Medicare taxes that employers withhold from employee paychecks. They are called "trust fund" taxes because employers hold these funds in trust for the government. The employer's share of Social Security and Medicare is not part of the trust fund.

Who can be held personally liable for the TFRP?

Any person who had responsibility and authority to collect, account for, and pay over the trust fund taxes and willfully failed to do so can be held liable. This often includes business owners, officers, directors, shareholders with operational control, payroll managers, bookkeepers, and even some employees with check-signing authority.

How much is the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty?

The TFRP equals 100% of the unpaid trust fund taxes. This includes the federal income tax withheld plus the employee portion of Social Security and Medicare taxes. It does not include the employer's share of FICA taxes, the employer's FUTA taxes, or any interest or penalties from the original business assessment.

Can I appeal a TFRP assessment?

Yes, you have several opportunities to dispute a TFRP. Before assessment, you can respond to the Letter 1153 (60-day letter) and request a conference with the IRS. After assessment, you can file an appeal with the IRS Office of Appeals, pay a small portion and sue for refund in federal court, or file for a Collection Due Process hearing if the IRS attempts to collect.

Can multiple people be held liable for the same TFRP?

Yes, the IRS can and often does assess the TFRP against multiple responsible persons for the same tax periods. However, the government can only collect the trust fund amount once in total. Payments by one responsible person reduce the liability for others. If you pay more than your share, you may have a right to contribution from other responsible persons.
