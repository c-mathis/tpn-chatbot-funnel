What Are IRS Allowable Expenses?

When the IRS evaluates your ability to pay tax debt, they use standardized expense allowances rather than your actual spending. Understanding these standards is critical for Offers in Compromise and installment agreements.

IRS allowable expenses, officially called Collection Financial Standards, are the amounts the IRS permits taxpayers to deduct from their income when calculating ability to pay. These standards determine how much of your income must go toward your tax debt versus necessary living expenses. They directly impact your Reasonable Collection Potential (RCP) for an Offer in Compromise and your monthly payment for an installment agreement.

Categories of Allowable Expenses

The IRS divides allowable expenses into three main categories:

National Standards

These are fixed amounts that apply nationwide regardless of where you live. They cover expenses that do not vary significantly by location:

  • Food: Groceries and dining out
  • Housekeeping supplies: Cleaning products, paper goods, etc.
  • Apparel and services: Clothing and laundry
  • Personal care products and services: Haircuts, toiletries, etc.
  • Miscellaneous: Reading materials, school supplies, etc.

National standard amounts vary by family size and income level. Higher-income taxpayers are allowed slightly higher amounts to reflect typical spending patterns, but there are maximum caps.

Local Standards

These amounts vary based on your geographic location and cover:

Housing and Utilities

This includes mortgage or rent, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, repairs, gas, electric, water, trash, and other utilities. The allowable amount depends on your county or metropolitan area and household size. High-cost areas like San Francisco, New York, and Los Angeles have higher standards than rural areas.

Transportation

Transportation standards have two components:

  • Ownership costs: Monthly loan or lease payments (with caps based on the number of vehicles)
  • Operating costs: Gas, insurance, maintenance, registration, and parking (varies by region)

If you have no car payment, you can only claim operating costs. The IRS allows one vehicle per taxpayer who needs to commute, plus an additional vehicle if necessary for production of income.

Other Necessary Expenses

These expenses must be justified and documented. They are not automatically allowed but can be included if you can prove necessity:

  • Health care: Health insurance premiums, out-of-pocket medical costs, prescriptions
  • Court-ordered payments: Child support, alimony, tax obligations other than the debt at issue
  • Child care: Daycare, after-school care necessary for work
  • Life insurance: Term life insurance (whole life cash value may be counted as an asset)
  • Education: Required for current employment or job training
  • Elderly or disabled care: If you support a dependent with special needs

How Standards Affect Your Calculations

Here is an example of how IRS standards work in practice:

Taxpayer Scenario

  • Family of 4 in Dallas, Texas
  • Gross monthly income: $7,500
  • Actual monthly expenses: $7,200

IRS Calculation Using Standards

Gross Monthly Income $7,500
Allowable Expenses:
National Standards (food, clothing, etc.) $1,882
Housing and Utilities (Dallas area) $2,189
Transportation (2 cars, ownership + operating) $1,476
Health Insurance $650
Child Care (documented) $800
Total Allowable Expenses $6,997
Monthly Disposable Income $503

Even though this family spends $7,200 per month (leaving only $300 left over), the IRS calculates they have $503 in monthly disposable income because some of their actual expenses exceed the standards.

Expenses the IRS Does NOT Allow

Certain expenses are typically not allowed when calculating your ability to pay:

  • Credit card payments: Unsecured debt payments are not allowable
  • Voluntary retirement contributions: 401k and IRA contributions beyond mandatory amounts
  • College tuition: For children (unless required for your employment)
  • Private school tuition: For K-12 education
  • Charitable contributions: Donations to charities or religious organizations
  • Entertainment: Cable TV (beyond basic), streaming services, vacations
  • Luxury items: Excessive housing, vehicles, or other spending beyond standards

Exceeding the Standards

In some cases, you can justify expenses that exceed the IRS standards:

  • Health conditions: Documented medical issues requiring higher expenses
  • Necessary for income: Expenses directly required to earn your income
  • Court orders: Legally mandated payments
  • Unique circumstances: Special situations with proper documentation

To claim above-standard expenses, you must provide documentation proving the expense is necessary and cannot be reduced. Working with a tax professional who understands how to present these cases can make a significant difference in your outcome.

Finding Current Standards

The IRS publishes Collection Financial Standards on their website (irs.gov). Standards are updated periodically to reflect changes in living costs. When preparing your Offer in Compromise or installment agreement request, always use the most current standards for accurate calculations.

Understanding allowable expenses is fundamental to any tax relief strategy. A proper analysis of your situation using current IRS standards helps ensure you do not offer to pay more than necessary while still presenting a compelling case that the IRS will accept.

Key Points to Remember

Standardized Amounts

The IRS uses fixed standards for expenses, not your actual spending, when calculating ability to pay.

Location Matters

Housing and transportation allowances vary by county. High-cost areas get higher allowances.

Documentation Required

Other necessary expenses like health care and child care must be documented and justified.

Some Expenses Excluded

Credit cards, voluntary retirement, and discretionary spending are not allowable expenses.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are IRS Collection Financial Standards?

IRS Collection Financial Standards are guidelines the IRS uses to determine how much a taxpayer can afford to pay toward tax debt. They set maximum allowable amounts for living expenses like housing, transportation, food, and personal care. These standards are used when evaluating Offers in Compromise, installment agreements, and currently not collectible status.

What is the difference between national and local standards?

National standards apply to expenses that do not vary significantly by location, such as food, clothing, housekeeping supplies, and personal care. Local standards apply to expenses that vary by geographic area, including housing and utilities, and transportation. Local standards are set by county or metropolitan statistical area.

What if my actual expenses exceed IRS standards?

If your actual expenses exceed IRS standards, the IRS may only allow the standard amount unless you can demonstrate that higher expenses are necessary for your health, welfare, or income production. Documented medical conditions, special circumstances, and necessary business expenses may justify amounts above the standards.

Are retirement contributions considered allowable expenses?

Generally, voluntary retirement contributions are not considered allowable expenses when calculating your ability to pay. The IRS expects you to redirect these funds toward your tax debt. However, mandatory retirement contributions required by your employer may be allowable. Existing retirement account balances are treated separately as assets.

How often do IRS expense standards change?

The IRS updates its Collection Financial Standards periodically, typically annually for national standards and as economic conditions change for local standards. When applying for tax relief, always use the most current standards available on the IRS website or through your tax professional.
