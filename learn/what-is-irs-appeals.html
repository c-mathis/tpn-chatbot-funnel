What Is IRS Appeals?

The IRS Office of Appeals provides an independent forum to resolve tax disputes without going to court. Understanding when and how to use this process can help you challenge unfavorable IRS decisions.

The IRS Office of Appeals is an independent organization within the IRS that helps resolve tax disputes between taxpayers and the IRS. Appeals Officers are separate from the IRS employees who made the original determination, allowing for an impartial review of your case. The Appeals process offers a faster, less expensive alternative to resolving disputes through the court system.

When to Use IRS Appeals

You can request an appeal in many situations where you disagree with an IRS decision:

  • Audit results: If you disagree with the changes proposed after an audit examination
  • Rejected Offer in Compromise: When the IRS denies your request to settle tax debt for less than owed
  • Denied installment agreement: If your payment plan request is rejected or terminated
  • Penalty assessments: When you believe penalties should be reduced or removed
  • Collection actions: Before or after the IRS files a lien or issues a levy
  • Trust fund recovery penalty: If you are held personally liable for employment taxes
  • Innocent spouse relief denial: When your claim for relief from joint liability is rejected

How the Appeals Process Works

Step 1: Request an Appeal

You must submit a written request within the timeframe specified in your IRS notice, typically 30 days. For smaller cases (under $25,000), you can use Form 12203, Request for Appeals Review. For larger cases or more complex issues, you will need to submit a formal written protest that includes your name, address, tax years involved, a list of disputed items, the facts supporting your position, and the law or authority you are relying on.

Step 2: Case Assignment

Your case is assigned to an Appeals Officer who reviews your file, the IRS's position, and your arguments. The Appeals Officer is trained to evaluate cases independently and has authority to settle disputes that the examining division does not have.

Step 3: Conference

The Appeals Officer will schedule a conference with you, which can be conducted by telephone, video conference, or in person. During the conference, you present your case, explain your position, and provide any additional documentation. You can represent yourself or have a qualified representative (enrolled agent, CPA, or attorney) appear on your behalf.

Step 4: Resolution

After reviewing all information, the Appeals Officer will propose a resolution. They can settle cases based on the "hazards of litigation," meaning they consider what might happen if the case went to court. If you agree with the proposed resolution, you sign an agreement and the case closes. If you disagree, you retain the right to take your case to Tax Court.

Types of Appeals Procedures

Collection Appeals Program (CAP)

CAP is an expedited process for disputing collection actions that have already occurred, such as a filed lien, levy on wages, or bank account seizure. CAP is faster but does not give you the right to go to Tax Court if you disagree with the decision.

Collection Due Process (CDP) Hearing

A CDP hearing provides more formal rights when the IRS proposes to levy your property or has filed a Notice of Federal Tax Lien. You must request a CDP hearing within 30 days of the notice. Unlike CAP, CDP preserves your right to petition Tax Court if you disagree with the outcome.

Fast Track Settlement

For cases still under examination, Fast Track Settlement allows you to work with an Appeals Officer while the audit is ongoing. This can resolve disputes more quickly without waiting for the examination to close.

Tips for a Successful Appeal

  • Meet all deadlines: Missing the appeal deadline can permanently waive your rights
  • Be organized: Present your arguments clearly with supporting documentation
  • Know the law: Cite relevant tax code sections, regulations, or court cases that support your position
  • Be professional: Treat the Appeals Officer with respect; they have authority to help resolve your case
  • Consider representation: A qualified tax professional can significantly improve your chances of success
  • Be realistic: Appeals Officers look for fair outcomes, not complete victories for either side

The Appeals process resolves the majority of cases without the need for litigation. By understanding your rights and preparing thoroughly, you can effectively dispute IRS decisions and work toward a fair resolution of your tax dispute.

Key Points to Remember

Independent Review

Appeals Officers are separate from the IRS employees who made the original decision, providing an impartial evaluation of your case.

No Filing Fee

There is no cost to file an appeal with the IRS Office of Appeals, making it accessible to all taxpayers.

30-Day Deadline

Most appeal requests must be filed within 30 days of the IRS decision. Missing this deadline can waive your appeal rights.

Settlement Authority

Appeals Officers can settle cases based on litigation hazards, often achieving better outcomes than the examination level.

Frequently Asked Questions

What decisions can I appeal to the IRS?

You can appeal most IRS decisions including audit findings, penalty assessments, rejected Offers in Compromise, denied installment agreements, collection actions like liens and levies, trust fund recovery penalty assessments, and employment tax determinations.

How long do I have to file an IRS appeal?

The deadline varies by situation. For audit results, you typically have 30 days from the date of the letter proposing changes. For a rejected Offer in Compromise, you have 30 days from the rejection date. For Collection Due Process hearings, you have 30 days from the date on the notice. Missing these deadlines can limit or eliminate your appeal rights.

Is the IRS Appeals process free?

Yes, there is no fee to file an appeal with the IRS Office of Appeals. However, you may incur costs if you hire a tax professional to represent you, which is often recommended for complex cases.

What happens during an IRS Appeals conference?

During an Appeals conference, you present your case to an Appeals Officer who is independent from the IRS division that made the original decision. The conference can be conducted by phone, video, or in person. The Appeals Officer reviews the facts, considers both sides, and works toward a fair resolution. They have authority to settle cases based on the hazards of litigation.

What if I disagree with the Appeals decision?

If you disagree with the Appeals decision, you have several options depending on your case. You can request mediation, file a petition with the U.S. Tax Court, or in some cases pursue the matter in federal district court or the Court of Federal Claims. Tax Court petitions must generally be filed within 90 days of receiving a Notice of Deficiency.
