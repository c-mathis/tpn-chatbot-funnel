An IRS Power of Attorney (POA) is a legal authorization that allows a qualified individual to represent you before the Internal Revenue Service. By signing Form 2848, you grant your representative the authority to receive confidential tax information, communicate with the IRS, and act on your behalf in tax matters for the specific tax years and tax types you designate.

Who Can Represent You Before the IRS

Not everyone can represent taxpayers before the IRS. Only certain practitioners have "unlimited representation rights," meaning they can represent clients in any matter before the IRS:

Enrolled Agents (EAs)

Enrolled agents are federally licensed tax practitioners who have passed a rigorous IRS examination covering individual and business taxes, or who have worked for the IRS for at least 5 years in a position requiring them to interpret and apply the tax code. EAs specialize in tax matters and can represent taxpayers before all administrative levels of the IRS.

Certified Public Accountants (CPAs)

CPAs are licensed by state boards of accountancy and have met education, examination, and experience requirements. While CPAs may have broad accounting expertise, not all specialize in tax representation. When choosing a CPA for tax relief, look for one with specific experience in IRS collections and resolution.

Attorneys

Licensed attorneys can represent clients before the IRS. Tax attorneys typically focus on complex tax matters, litigation, and cases that may proceed to Tax Court. They are particularly valuable in situations involving fraud allegations, criminal tax matters, or complex legal issues.

Limited Representation Rights

Some individuals have limited rights to represent taxpayers:

  • Unenrolled return preparers: Can represent taxpayers only for returns they prepared and signed, and only before revenue agents, customer service representatives, and similar IRS employees
  • VITA/TCE volunteers: Have limited representation rights for returns prepared through these programs
  • Annual Filing Season Program participants: Have limited representation rights similar to unenrolled preparers

Understanding Form 2848

Form 2848, Power of Attorney and Declaration of Representative, is the official IRS form used to authorize representation. Key components include:

Taxpayer Information

Your name, address, Social Security number or EIN, and contact information.

Representative Information

Your representative's name, address, CAF (Centralized Authorization File) number, PTIN, telephone, and fax numbers. The representative must sign the form declaring their authorization to practice before the IRS.

Tax Matters

You must specify the type of tax (income, employment, estate, etc.) and the tax years or periods covered. The POA only applies to the matters you list. If you want representation for multiple years or tax types, each must be specified.

Acts Authorized

Standard authority includes receiving confidential information, representing you at IRS meetings and hearings, and signing documents on your behalf for certain matters. You can grant additional authorities or restrict the standard authority if desired.

Specific Acts Not Authorized by Default

Unless you specifically authorize them, your representative cannot:

  • Sign your income tax return
  • Receive refund checks
  • Endorse refund checks
  • Execute waivers extending the statutory period for assessment or collection
  • Execute closing agreements

Form 2848 vs. Form 8821

It is important to understand the difference between these two authorization forms:

Feature Form 2848 (POA) Form 8821 (TIA)
Receive tax information Yes Yes
Speak with IRS on your behalf Yes No
Represent in audits/appeals Yes No
Sign agreements on your behalf Yes (if authorized) No
Negotiate with IRS Yes No

Form 8821 is often used when someone needs access to your tax information but will not be representing you, such as a financial planner or mortgage broker.

Filing and Managing Your POA

Submitting Form 2848

Form 2848 can be submitted by fax to the IRS CAF unit, by mail, or electronically through the IRS e-Services portal (only available to practitioners). Once processed, the POA is entered into the IRS Centralized Authorization File, and the representative can begin acting on your behalf.

Revoking a Power of Attorney

You can revoke a POA at any time by submitting a written statement to the IRS indicating that you are revoking all prior POAs for the specified tax matters. You can also revoke a POA by filing a new Form 2848 that does not include the previous representative.

Multiple Representatives

You can authorize multiple representatives on the same Form 2848 or file separate forms for different representatives. This is common when working with a tax resolution firm where several professionals may need to communicate with the IRS about your case.

Benefits of Professional Representation

Having a qualified representative handle IRS matters offers several advantages:

  • Expert communication: Tax professionals know how to communicate effectively with the IRS and understand the procedural requirements
  • Time savings: Your representative handles calls, correspondence, and meetings so you do not have to spend hours on hold
  • Better outcomes: Experienced representatives often achieve better results in audits, appeals, and collection negotiations
  • Reduced stress: Let a professional handle the stressful interactions with the IRS
  • Protection of rights: Representatives ensure your taxpayer rights are protected throughout the process

Key Points to Remember

Qualified Representatives

Only enrolled agents, CPAs, and attorneys have unlimited representation rights before the IRS.

Form 2848

This is the official form to authorize representation. It must specify the tax years and tax types covered.

You Stay in Control

A POA does not transfer your authority. You can revoke it anytime and make all final decisions.

Better Outcomes

Professional representation typically leads to better results in audits, appeals, and collection matters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who can represent me before the IRS?

Only certain individuals can represent you before the IRS: enrolled agents (EAs), certified public accountants (CPAs), and attorneys. These practitioners have unlimited representation rights. Others, like unenrolled return preparers, have limited representation rights only for returns they prepared.

What is the difference between Form 2848 and Form 8821?

Form 2848 (Power of Attorney) allows your representative to speak and act on your behalf with the IRS, including representing you in audits, appeals, and collection matters. Form 8821 (Tax Information Authorization) only allows someone to receive and inspect your tax information but not represent you or speak to the IRS on your behalf.

How long does a Power of Attorney last?

A Power of Attorney remains in effect until you revoke it, your representative withdraws, or 7 years pass from the date you sign it. You can specify an earlier expiration date on the form. You can revoke a POA at any time by filing a written statement with the IRS or by filing a new Form 2848.

Can I have multiple representatives on one Power of Attorney?

Yes, you can name multiple representatives on Form 2848. All named representatives will have the same authority unless you specify otherwise. This is common when working with a tax resolution firm where multiple professionals may need to communicate with the IRS on your case.

Does signing a Power of Attorney mean I give up control?

No. Signing Form 2848 does not transfer your tax responsibilities or decision-making authority. You retain the right to participate in any meeting with the IRS, make final decisions about your case, and revoke the POA at any time. Your representative acts on your behalf but you remain in control.
