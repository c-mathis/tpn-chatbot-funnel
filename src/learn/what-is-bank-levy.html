A bank levy is one of the most disruptive collection actions the IRS can take. Without warning, you may find your bank account frozen and inaccessible. Understanding how bank levies work and what options you have is critical to protecting your finances and finding a resolution.

How Does an IRS Bank Levy Work?

When the IRS issues a bank levy, here's what happens:

  1. IRS identifies your bank: The IRS knows where you bank from your tax returns, W-2s, 1099s, and other financial records
  2. Levy notice sent: The IRS sends Form 668-A (Notice of Levy) to your bank
  3. Immediate freeze: Upon receiving the notice, your bank must immediately freeze funds in your account
  4. 21-day holding period: The bank holds your frozen funds for 21 days
  5. Funds sent to IRS: After 21 days, if the levy hasn't been released, the bank sends the frozen amount to the IRS

The freeze happens on the day the bank receives the levy. Any money in your account at that moment (up to the amount you owe) is captured. You cannot access these frozen funds during the holding period.

The 21-Day Holding Period

The 21-day hold is a crucial window of opportunity. During this time:

  • Your money is frozen but hasn't been sent to the IRS yet
  • You can work with the IRS to resolve the issue
  • You can prove that certain funds are exempt
  • You can set up a payment arrangement that might release the levy
  • You can demonstrate economic hardship

Act immediately. Once the 21 days pass and funds are sent to the IRS, getting your money back is extremely difficult.

What Does a Bank Levy Capture?

What Is Taken

  • Checking account balances
  • Savings account balances
  • Money market accounts
  • Certificates of deposit (CDs)
  • Joint accounts (even if only one account holder owes taxes)
  • Business accounts (if you owe as an individual and the account is in your name)

What May Be Exempt

Certain funds in your account may be exempt from levy:

  • Social Security benefits (if identifiable and deposited within the past 2 months)
  • Federal benefit payments (VA benefits, SSI, etc.)
  • Workers' compensation payments
  • Certain pension and retirement payments
  • Unemployment compensation

If your account contains exempt funds, you must act quickly to notify both the IRS and your bank. Provide documentation showing the source of the funds.

Bank Levy vs. Wage Levy

Understanding the difference is important:

Bank Levy

  • One-time seizure of funds on the date received
  • Captures whatever is in your account at that moment
  • Doesn't automatically take future deposits
  • 21-day holding period before funds go to IRS

Wage Levy

  • Continuous - takes from every paycheck
  • Continues until released or debt paid
  • No holding period - each payment goes directly to IRS
  • Follows you if you change jobs

The IRS can issue multiple bank levies over time. Each one captures funds at the moment it's received by the bank.

How to Release or Prevent a Bank Levy

During the 21-Day Period

If your account has been levied, you have 21 days to act:

  1. Contact the IRS immediately: Call the number on your levy notice or work with a tax professional
  2. Set up a payment arrangement: Entering an installment agreement can result in levy release
  3. Prove hardship: If the levy prevents you from meeting basic needs, document this for the IRS
  4. Identify exempt funds: If your account contains Social Security or other exempt payments, provide proof
  5. Submit an Offer in Compromise: Starting the OIC process may lead to levy release
  6. Request Currently Not Collectible status: If you can't afford any payments, CNC status releases levies

Preventing Future Bank Levies

  • Don't ignore IRS notices: Respond to every letter you receive
  • Set up a payment plan: Being in an installment agreement prevents most levies
  • File all tax returns: The IRS often won't negotiate until you're current on filings
  • Stay in communication: Proactive contact with the IRS shows good faith

What to Do If You've Been Levied

Time is critical. Here's your action plan:

  1. Review the levy notice: Understand how much the IRS claims you owe
  2. Check for exempt funds: Identify any Social Security or other protected deposits
  3. Contact your bank: Confirm the amount frozen and the 21-day timeline
  4. Gather financial documents: Bank statements, income proof, expense documentation
  5. Contact the IRS or a tax professional: Begin working on a resolution immediately
  6. Explore all options: Payment plan, hardship relief, OIC, or CNC status

Key Points to Remember

21-Day Window

You have 21 days from the levy date to resolve the issue before funds are sent to the IRS. Act immediately.

One-Time Capture

A bank levy captures funds at a single moment in time. It doesn't automatically take future deposits.

Some Funds Exempt

Social Security, federal benefits, and certain other payments may be exempt from bank levy if properly identified.

Release Possible

Levies can be released through payment plans, hardship claims, exempt funds, or other resolution options.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the 21-day hold on a bank levy?

When the IRS issues a bank levy, your bank must freeze the funds in your account but cannot send them to the IRS for 21 days. This holding period is designed to give you time to resolve the issue - either by working out an agreement with the IRS or proving that some funds are exempt. After 21 days, if the levy hasn't been released, the bank must send the frozen funds to the IRS.

Will a bank levy take my entire account balance?

The bank levy will freeze funds up to the amount you owe the IRS. If your account balance is less than your tax debt, your entire balance could be frozen and sent to the IRS. If you have more than you owe, only the amount needed to cover your debt (plus any fees) will be taken. Joint accounts are also subject to levy for one spouse's tax debt.

Can the IRS levy my bank account without notice?

No, the IRS must follow specific procedures before issuing a bank levy. You must receive a bill, a notice of intent to levy (usually CP504), and at least 30 days to respond. However, many people miss or ignore these notices. If you've received IRS letters you haven't opened or addressed, a bank levy could occur. In rare emergency situations (jeopardy levies), the IRS can act without normal notice requirements.

Can the IRS levy money deposited after the levy date?

A bank levy captures funds in your account at the moment the bank receives the levy notice. It does not automatically capture future deposits. However, the IRS can issue additional levies. If you deposit money after a levy is processed, that money is safe from that specific levy - but the IRS could issue a new levy at any time, freezing newly deposited funds.

How do I get a bank levy released?

You can get a bank levy released by: 1) Paying the full tax debt, 2) Setting up an installment agreement with the IRS, 3) Proving economic hardship, 4) Showing the levy was issued in error, 5) Having your account placed in Currently Not Collectible status, or 6) Proving certain funds are exempt (like Social Security). Act quickly during the 21-day hold period - once funds are sent to the IRS, recovery is much more difficult.
