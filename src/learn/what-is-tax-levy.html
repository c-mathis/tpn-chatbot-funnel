A tax levy is one of the most aggressive collection actions the IRS can take against you. When the IRS issues a levy, they can legally seize your property, including money in your bank accounts, portions of your paycheck, your car, and even your home. Understanding how levies work is essential for protecting your assets and finding a resolution.

How Does a Tax Levy Work?

Before the IRS can levy your property, they must follow specific legal procedures:

  1. Assessment: The IRS assesses the tax you owe
  2. Notice and demand: You receive a bill for the amount owed
  3. Neglect or refusal: You fail to pay within the time stated
  4. Final Notice: The IRS sends CP504 or Letter 1058 - Final Notice of Intent to Levy and Notice of Your Right to a Hearing
  5. 30-day waiting period: You have 30 days to respond before levy action

If you don't respond or resolve the debt during this period, the IRS can begin seizing your assets.

Types of Tax Levies

Bank Levy

The IRS contacts your bank and instructs them to freeze your accounts. The bank holds the funds for 21 days, then sends them to the IRS. This 21-day period gives you time to resolve the issue or prove exemption. The levy captures the account balance on the day it's received - it doesn't automatically take future deposits (though the IRS can issue additional levies).

Wage Levy (Wage Garnishment)

A wage levy is a continuous levy that takes a portion of each paycheck until the debt is satisfied or the levy is released. Your employer is legally required to comply. The amount taken depends on your filing status and number of dependents - the IRS allows you to keep only the minimum needed for basic living expenses.

Property Levy

The IRS can seize and sell your physical property, including vehicles, boats, real estate, and other valuables. Property seizures are less common and typically reserved for larger debts or when other collection methods fail.

Accounts Receivable Levy

For business owners, the IRS can levy money owed to you by your customers or clients. They contact your customers directly and instruct them to send payments to the IRS instead of you.

What the IRS Cannot Levy

Certain assets are protected from levy:

  • Unemployment benefits
  • Certain annuity and pension payments
  • Workers' compensation
  • Certain public assistance payments
  • Minimum exempt amount of wages, salary, and other income
  • Necessary clothing and schoolbooks
  • Fuel, provisions, furniture, and personal effects (up to $9,170)
  • Tools of your trade, books, and equipment (up to $4,600)
  • Certain service-connected disability payments

How to Stop or Release a Levy

You have several options to stop or release a levy:

Pay in Full

The most direct solution. Paying your tax debt in full immediately releases all levies.

Installment Agreement

Setting up a payment plan with the IRS will typically result in levy release. Once you're in an approved installment agreement, the IRS generally won't levy your assets as long as you remain compliant.

Offer in Compromise

If you qualify, submitting an OIC can stop levy actions while your offer is being considered. If accepted, the debt is settled for less than owed.

Currently Not Collectible Status

If paying the tax debt would cause economic hardship, the IRS may place your account in CNC status and release the levy.

Collection Due Process Hearing

If you received a Final Notice of Intent to Levy, you have 30 days to request a Collection Due Process (CDP) hearing. This stops levy action while your case is reviewed.

Prove Economic Hardship

If the levy is causing immediate economic hardship - preventing you from meeting basic living expenses - the IRS may release it.

Appeal

If the levy was issued incorrectly or you have grounds for appeal, you can challenge the levy through the IRS appeals process.

What to Do If You Receive a Levy Notice

Time is critical when dealing with levy notices:

  1. Don't ignore it: The IRS will follow through if you don't respond
  2. Review your options: Determine if you can pay, need a payment plan, or qualify for other relief
  3. Request a CDP hearing: If within the 30-day window, this pauses levy action
  4. Contact the IRS: Call the number on your notice to discuss resolution
  5. Seek professional help: A tax professional can negotiate on your behalf and often achieve better outcomes

Key Points to Remember

Actual Seizure

Unlike a lien, a levy actually takes your property. The IRS can seize bank accounts, wages, and physical assets.

21-Day Hold

Bank levies hold your funds for 21 days before sending to the IRS, giving you time to resolve the issue.

Warning Required

The IRS must send you a Final Notice and give you 30 days to respond before issuing a levy.

Can Be Released

Levies can be stopped through payment plans, OIC, CNC status, or proving economic hardship.

Frequently Asked Questions

What assets can the IRS levy?

The IRS can levy almost any asset you own, including: bank accounts, wages and salary, retirement accounts (in some cases), Social Security benefits, accounts receivable, rental income, commissions, the cash value of life insurance, dividends, and physical property like cars, boats, and real estate. However, certain items are exempt, such as necessary clothing, essential household items, tools needed for work (up to a limit), and unemployment benefits.

How do I stop an IRS levy?

You can stop or release a levy by: 1) Paying your tax debt in full, 2) Setting up an installment agreement, 3) Having your account placed in Currently Not Collectible status, 4) Submitting an Offer in Compromise, 5) Filing for bankruptcy (automatic stay), or 6) Proving the levy is creating an economic hardship. Contact the IRS or a tax professional immediately if you receive a levy notice.

What is the difference between a continuous levy and a one-time levy?

A one-time levy seizes assets at a single point in time, like taking the balance in your bank account on the day the levy is received. A continuous levy keeps taking payments over time, most commonly used for wages. Once a wage levy is in place, your employer must send a portion of each paycheck to the IRS until the levy is released or the debt is paid.

Can the IRS levy my bank account without warning?

No, the IRS must follow specific procedures before issuing a levy. They must send you a bill for the amount owed, give you a notice of intent to levy (Final Notice of Intent to Levy and Notice of Your Right to a Hearing, typically CP504), and wait 30 days before levying. You have the right to request a Collection Due Process hearing within those 30 days.

Will a bank levy take all the money in my account?

A bank levy freezes the funds in your account(s) up to the amount you owe. The bank holds these funds for 21 days before sending them to the IRS. This 21-day window allows time to resolve the issue or prove the funds are exempt. If your balance is less than what you owe, the entire balance may be taken. Joint accounts can also be levied for one spouse's tax debt.
