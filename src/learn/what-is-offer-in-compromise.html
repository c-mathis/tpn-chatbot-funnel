If you owe more in back taxes than you can realistically pay, an Offer in Compromise may provide a path to financial relief. This IRS program allows certain taxpayers to negotiate a settlement for less than their total tax liability, effectively providing a fresh start.

How Does an Offer in Compromise Work?

An Offer in Compromise is essentially a negotiation between you and the IRS. You propose to pay a specific amount that you can afford, and if the IRS determines this represents the most they can reasonably expect to collect, they may accept your offer and forgive the remaining balance.

The IRS evaluates your offer based on your Reasonable Collection Potential (RCP), which is calculated using a specific formula that considers:

  • Your monthly disposable income (income minus allowable living expenses)
  • The equity in your assets (home, vehicles, investments, etc.)
  • Your future earning potential over the collection period
  • The remaining time the IRS has to collect the debt (typically 10 years from assessment)

Three Types of Offers in Compromise

The IRS accepts offers based on three different grounds:

1. Doubt as to Collectibility

This is the most common type. It applies when your assets and income are insufficient to pay the full tax debt. The IRS looks at what they can realistically collect from you and may accept less than the full amount.

2. Doubt as to Liability

This applies when there is a genuine dispute about whether you actually owe the tax debt, or the amount is incorrect. You must provide evidence supporting your position that the tax was assessed in error.

3. Effective Tax Administration

Even if you could technically pay the full amount, the IRS may accept a compromise if collecting would create economic hardship or would be unfair and inequitable due to exceptional circumstances.

Who Qualifies for an Offer in Compromise?

To be eligible for an OIC, you must meet several requirements:

  • All tax returns filed: You must have filed all required tax returns
  • Current on payments: If self-employed, you must be current on estimated tax payments for the current year
  • Not in bankruptcy: You cannot have an open bankruptcy case
  • Valid extension: If you filed an extension, you must have submitted that return

Beyond these basic requirements, good OIC candidates typically have limited income and assets relative to their tax debt, have experienced financial hardship, or face circumstances that make full payment genuinely impossible.

The OIC Application Process

Applying for an Offer in Compromise involves several steps:

  1. Pre-qualification: Use the IRS Pre-Qualifier tool or work with a tax professional to assess your eligibility
  2. Gather documentation: Collect financial records including income statements, bank statements, asset valuations, and expense documentation
  3. Complete required forms: Submit Form 656 (Offer in Compromise) and Form 433-A (OIC) or 433-B (OIC) for businesses
  4. Pay application fee: Include the $205 application fee (waived for low-income applicants)
  5. Submit initial payment: Include 20% of your offer amount for lump-sum offers, or the first month's payment for periodic payment offers
  6. Wait for review: The IRS will review your application and may request additional information

What Happens After Your Offer Is Accepted?

Once the IRS accepts your Offer in Compromise, you must comply with specific requirements for five years. You must file all tax returns on time and pay all taxes owed during this period. Failure to comply can result in your offer being defaulted, reinstating the full original debt.

The good news is that your remaining tax debt is fully forgiven once you complete the terms of your agreement. This provides genuine relief and allows you to move forward without the burden of unmanageable tax debt.

Key Points to Remember

Settle for Less

Qualifying taxpayers can settle their IRS debt for significantly less than the full amount owed, sometimes pennies on the dollar.

Collection Pause

While your OIC is under review, the IRS generally suspends collection actions including levies, liens, and garnishments.

Requirements Apply

You must have filed all tax returns, be current on estimated payments, and not be in bankruptcy to qualify for an OIC.

5-Year Compliance

After acceptance, you must stay tax compliant for 5 years or the offer can be defaulted and original debt reinstated.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can I settle my tax debt for with an OIC?

The settlement amount depends on your Reasonable Collection Potential (RCP), which considers your income, expenses, assets, and future earning potential. Some taxpayers settle for as little as 10-20 cents on the dollar, while others may settle for 50% or more. The IRS will only accept an amount they believe represents the most they can collect from you.

What are the three types of Offer in Compromise?

The IRS offers three types: 1) Doubt as to Collectibility - when you can't pay the full amount owed, 2) Doubt as to Liability - when there's a legitimate dispute about whether you owe the tax, and 3) Effective Tax Administration - when you can pay but doing so would create economic hardship or would be unfair.

How long does it take the IRS to process an Offer in Compromise?

The IRS typically takes 6-12 months to process an OIC application, though complex cases may take longer. During this time, collection actions are generally suspended, giving you relief from levies and garnishments while your offer is under review.

What happens if my Offer in Compromise is rejected?

If rejected, you have 30 days to file an appeal with the IRS Office of Appeals. You can also submit a new offer with updated information, or explore alternative options like an installment agreement or Currently Not Collectible status. Working with a tax professional can help identify why the offer was rejected and improve your chances on appeal.

Do I need to be current on my tax filings to apply for an OIC?

Yes, you must have filed all required tax returns before the IRS will consider your Offer in Compromise. If you have unfiled returns, you'll need to file them first. Additionally, you must be current on estimated tax payments if you're self-employed, and you cannot be in an open bankruptcy proceeding.
