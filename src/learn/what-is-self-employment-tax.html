Self-employment tax (SE tax) is how freelancers, independent contractors, and business owners pay into Social Security and Medicare. When you work for an employer, these taxes are split: you pay 7.65% through payroll withholding, and your employer pays the other 7.65%. When you're self-employed, you pay both halves, totaling 15.3%.

Breaking Down the 15.3% Rate

Self-employment tax consists of two components:

Social Security (12.4%): This portion funds retirement, disability, and survivor benefits. It only applies to earnings up to the Social Security wage base, which is $168,600 for 2024. Income above this threshold is not subject to the Social Security portion.

Medicare (2.9%): This funds Medicare health insurance. Unlike Social Security, there is no cap on Medicare tax, so it applies to all your self-employment income.

Additional Medicare Tax (0.9%): High earners pay an extra 0.9% Medicare tax on self-employment income exceeding $200,000 ($250,000 if married filing jointly). This brings the total Medicare rate to 3.8% on income above these thresholds.

Who Must Pay Self-Employment Tax?

You're subject to SE tax if you have net self-employment earnings of $400 or more. This includes:

  • Freelancers and independent contractors: Anyone working on a 1099 basis
  • Sole proprietors: Unincorporated businesses you own and operate
  • General partners: Your share of partnership income
  • LLC members: If your LLC is taxed as a sole proprietorship or partnership
  • Gig economy workers: Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart, Etsy sellers, etc.
  • Side hustlers: Part-time freelance work in addition to a W-2 job

S corporation shareholders are an exception. If your business is structured as an S corp, you pay yourself a "reasonable salary" subject to payroll taxes. Any additional distributions are not subject to SE tax, which is why some self-employed individuals choose this structure.

How to Calculate Self-Employment Tax

The calculation involves a few steps:

Step 1: Calculate your net self-employment income. This is your gross business income minus all business expenses (reported on Schedule C for sole proprietors).

Step 2: Multiply by 92.35% (0.9235). This adjustment simulates the employer/employee split and ensures you're not paying SE tax on the "employer" portion.

Step 3: Apply the 15.3% rate (or split between Social Security and Medicare if your income exceeds the Social Security wage base).

Example: If you have $80,000 net self-employment income:

  • $80,000 x 0.9235 = $73,880 taxable amount
  • $73,880 x 0.153 = $11,304 self-employment tax

You report this on Schedule SE, which is filed with your Form 1040.

The Self-Employment Tax Deduction

Here's some good news: You can deduct half of your SE tax as an adjustment to income. This is an "above-the-line" deduction, meaning you get it even if you don't itemize.

This deduction exists because employees don't pay income tax on their employer's half of FICA taxes. The deduction puts self-employed individuals on more equal footing. In the example above, you'd deduct $5,652 (half of $11,304), reducing your adjusted gross income and overall income tax.

Important: This deduction reduces your income tax but does NOT reduce the SE tax itself. The SE tax is still calculated on your full net self-employment income.

SE Tax vs. Income Tax: Understanding Both

Many new freelancers are surprised by their tax bills because they think of SE tax and income tax as the same thing. They're not:

Self-employment tax (15.3%): A flat tax on your net self-employment income for Social Security and Medicare. There's no standard deduction or exemption that reduces this.

Income tax (10-37%): Graduated tax on your total taxable income (from all sources) after deductions. Your business profit flows to your personal return and is taxed at your marginal rate.

With $80,000 in self-employment income, you might pay $11,304 in SE tax PLUS $8,000-12,000 in income tax (depending on deductions and other factors). That's why self-employed individuals should set aside 25-30% of income for taxes.

Paying Self-Employment Tax Throughout the Year

Because no employer is withholding taxes from your payments, you're responsible for paying estimated taxes quarterly. The IRS expects to receive payments throughout the year, not just at filing time.

If you don't make quarterly payments and owe more than $1,000 at filing time, you'll face underpayment penalties. This is one of the most common ways self-employed individuals get into tax debt.

Reducing Your Self-Employment Tax Burden

Several strategies can help minimize SE tax:

  • Maximize business deductions: Lower net income means lower SE tax. Track all legitimate expenses.
  • Consider S corp election: For higher earners, paying yourself a salary and taking distributions can reduce SE tax (but comes with added complexity).
  • Retirement contributions: SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, or solo 401(k) contributions reduce taxable income (though not SE tax directly).
  • Health insurance deduction: Self-employed health insurance premiums are deductible, reducing income tax.
  • Qualified Business Income deduction: The 20% QBI deduction reduces income tax on qualified business income.

If you're struggling with self-employment taxes or have gotten behind, professional help can get you back on track and optimize your tax situation going forward.

Key Points to Remember

15.3% Total Rate

Self-employed pay double what W-2 employees pay: 12.4% for Social Security plus 2.9% for Medicare.

Half Is Deductible

You can deduct 50% of your SE tax as an adjustment to income, reducing your overall tax burden.

$400 Threshold

SE tax applies when net self-employment income reaches $400 or more in a year.

In Addition to Income Tax

SE tax is separate from income tax. You pay both on your self-employment earnings.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the self-employment tax rate?

The self-employment tax rate is 15.3%, consisting of 12.4% for Social Security (on earnings up to $168,600 in 2024) and 2.9% for Medicare (on all earnings). High earners pay an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax on self-employment income over $200,000 ($250,000 for married filing jointly). This rate is double what W-2 employees pay because you're covering both the employee and employer portions.

Who has to pay self-employment tax?

You must pay self-employment tax if you have net self-employment earnings of $400 or more. This includes freelancers and independent contractors, sole proprietors, general partners in partnerships, members of LLCs taxed as sole props or partnerships, and gig workers (Uber, DoorDash, Etsy sellers, etc.). S corporation shareholders who receive distributions don't pay SE tax on those distributions, only on their salary.

Can I deduct self-employment tax?

Yes, you can deduct half of your self-employment tax as an adjustment to income (above-the-line deduction). This is because employees only pay half of FICA taxes while employers pay the other half and deduct it as a business expense. The deduction reduces your adjusted gross income but does not reduce your self-employment income for calculating the SE tax itself.

How do I calculate self-employment tax?

First, calculate your net self-employment income (gross income minus business expenses). Multiply by 0.9235 (this accounts for the employer-equivalent portion deduction). Then multiply by 15.3% (or use the split rates: 12.4% Social Security up to the limit plus 2.9% Medicare on all). Use Schedule SE with your tax return. Example: $100,000 net income x 0.9235 = $92,350 x 15.3% = $14,130 SE tax.

Do I pay self-employment tax in addition to income tax?

Yes, self-employment tax is separate from and in addition to federal income tax. If you're self-employed, you pay both: SE tax (15.3%) for Social Security and Medicare, plus income tax (10-37% depending on bracket) on your net business profit. This is why self-employed individuals often have higher total tax burdens than W-2 employees with the same gross income.
