IRS wage garnishment, officially called a wage levy, is one of the most impactful collection actions the IRS can take. Unlike a bank levy that seizes funds once, a wage levy continuously takes money from each paycheck until your debt is resolved. This can severely affect your ability to pay bills and support your family.

How Does IRS Wage Garnishment Work?

When the IRS issues a wage levy:

  1. Notice to employer: The IRS sends Form 668-W (Notice of Levy on Wages) to your employer
  2. Employer compliance: Your employer is legally required to comply immediately
  3. You complete Part 3: You receive Part 3 of the form to claim exemptions (filing status and dependents)
  4. Exempt amount calculated: Based on your exemptions, an amount is protected from garnishment
  5. Continuous deduction: Your employer sends everything above the exempt amount to the IRS each pay period

The wage levy continues automatically with each paycheck. Your employer doesn't need new notices - the original levy stays in effect until released.

How Much Can the IRS Take?

The IRS uses Publication 1494 to determine how much of your paycheck is exempt from levy. The exempt amount depends on:

  • Your filing status (single, married filing jointly, head of household)
  • Number of dependents you claim
  • Your pay frequency (weekly, biweekly, monthly)

Important: If you fail to return Part 3 of the levy notice to your employer, they must calculate your exemption as married filing separately with zero dependents - the lowest possible exempt amount.

IRS vs. Regular Creditor Garnishment

IRS wage garnishment is significantly more severe than garnishment by regular creditors:

Regular Creditors

  • Limited to 25% of disposable earnings
  • Requires a court judgment first
  • Subject to state law limitations
  • You receive court notices and can contest

IRS Wage Levy

  • Can take everything above a small exempt amount (often 70%+ of pay)
  • No court judgment required
  • Federal power overrides most state protections
  • Administrative process with limited challenges

What Triggers a Wage Levy?

The IRS doesn't issue wage levies without warning. Before garnishing wages, the IRS must:

  • Assess the tax and send you a notice and demand for payment
  • You must have neglected or refused to pay
  • Send a Final Notice of Intent to Levy (CP504 or Letter 1058)
  • Wait at least 30 days after the final notice

If you've received these notices and haven't responded or resolved the debt, the IRS can proceed with garnishing your wages.

How to Stop or Release a Wage Garnishment

1. Pay the Full Amount

Paying your tax debt in full immediately releases the wage levy. This is the most straightforward solution if you have the funds available.

2. Set Up an Installment Agreement

Entering into a payment plan with the IRS typically results in wage levy release. Once you're in an approved installment agreement and making payments, the IRS will release the garnishment.

3. Request Currently Not Collectible Status

If paying your tax debt would cause economic hardship, the IRS may place your account in CNC status and release the levy. You'll need to prove you can't afford basic living expenses.

4. Submit an Offer in Compromise

Filing an OIC doesn't automatically stop a levy, but once your offer is accepted, the levy is released. You may be able to negotiate levy release while your offer is being reviewed.

5. Demonstrate Economic Hardship

If the wage levy is preventing you from meeting basic necessities - food, housing, utilities, medical care - you can request release based on economic hardship. You'll need to document your expenses.

6. Request a Collection Due Process Hearing

If you received a Final Notice of Intent to Levy and haven't yet had your wages garnished, you have 30 days to request a CDP hearing. This pauses levy action while your case is reviewed.

What to Do If Your Wages Are Being Garnished

  1. Complete Part 3 immediately: Submit your filing status and dependent information to maximize your exempt amount
  2. Contact the IRS: Call the number on your levy notice to discuss resolution options
  3. Gather financial information: Document your income, expenses, and assets
  4. Explore your options: Determine if you qualify for an installment agreement, OIC, or CNC status
  5. Consider professional help: A tax professional can negotiate with the IRS and often achieve faster levy release

Key Points to Remember

Severe Impact

IRS wage levies can take 70% or more of your paycheck, leaving only a small exempt amount for living expenses.

Continuous Action

Unlike bank levies, wage garnishment continues with every paycheck until your debt is paid or the levy released.

Part 3 Matters

Completing Form 668-W Part 3 with your dependents can significantly increase how much of your paycheck you keep.

Release Options

You can stop wage garnishment through payment plans, OIC, CNC status, or proving economic hardship.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can the IRS garnish from my paycheck?

The IRS determines exempt amounts using Publication 1494 tables based on your filing status and number of dependents. The exempt amount is typically equivalent to the standard deduction plus personal exemption amounts divided by pay periods. For example, a single person with no dependents might keep only about $1,000-1,200 per month, with the rest going to the IRS. The more dependents you claim, the higher your exempt amount.

Is IRS wage garnishment different from regular wage garnishment?

Yes, IRS wage garnishment (called a wage levy) is more aggressive than regular creditor garnishments. Regular creditors can typically only take 25% of disposable income. The IRS can take much more - everything above a small exempt amount needed for basic living expenses. Additionally, the IRS doesn't need a court order; they can levy directly after following required notice procedures.

How long does an IRS wage garnishment last?

An IRS wage levy continues until one of the following occurs: 1) Your tax debt is paid in full, 2) The collection statute expires (typically 10 years), 3) You set up an approved payment arrangement, 4) The IRS releases the levy for another reason (hardship, error, etc.), or 5) You reach a settlement through an Offer in Compromise. The levy doesn't automatically stop when you change jobs - the IRS can send a new levy to your new employer.

Can I stop an IRS wage garnishment?

Yes, you can stop a wage garnishment by: 1) Paying the full tax debt, 2) Setting up an installment agreement with the IRS, 3) Being placed in Currently Not Collectible status, 4) Having an Offer in Compromise accepted, 5) Filing for bankruptcy (automatic stay), or 6) Demonstrating that the levy causes economic hardship. Acting quickly is essential - contact the IRS or a tax professional immediately.

Will my employer fire me if the IRS garnishes my wages?

While some employers may view a wage garnishment negatively, federal law prohibits employers from firing you for a single wage garnishment. However, this protection is limited to one garnishment. If you have multiple garnishments from different creditors, the protection may not apply. Many employers understand financial difficulties happen and won't take adverse action. The garnishment is a legal matter your employer must comply with.
