What Is IRS Form 941?

IRS Form 941, officially titled "Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return," is the form businesses use to report wages paid to employees, tips employees reported, federal income tax withheld, and both employer and employee portions of Social Security and Medicare taxes. If you have employees, Form 941 is a critical compliance requirement that you'll file four times per year.

These payroll taxes are often called "employment taxes" or "941 taxes." The IRS takes these taxes extremely seriously because a portion represents money held in trust for the government - taxes withheld from your employees' paychecks that were never really yours to begin with.

Who Must File Form 941?

Generally, any employer who pays wages to employees must file Form 941, with some exceptions:

  • Must file 941: Businesses with employees who work regularly, including corporations, partnerships, LLCs, and sole proprietors with employees.
  • Exception - Form 944: Very small employers (annual employment tax liability of $1,000 or less) may be eligible to file Form 944 annually instead.
  • Exception - Form 943: Agricultural employers file Form 943 for farm workers.
  • Exception - Household employers: If you only have household employees, you report their taxes on Schedule H with your personal Form 1040.

What Taxes Does Form 941 Cover?

Federal Income Tax Withholding

The income tax you withhold from employee wages based on their W-4 form. This amount varies by employee based on their filing status, dependents, and any additional withholding they request.

Social Security Tax

The current Social Security tax rate is 12.4% (6.2% employee share + 6.2% employer share) on wages up to the annual wage base ($168,600 in 2024). You report both portions on Form 941.

Medicare Tax

The Medicare tax rate is 2.9% (1.45% employee share + 1.45% employer share) on all wages with no cap. Additionally, employees earning over $200,000 pay an extra 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax (no employer match).

Understanding the Quarterly Filing Schedule

Form 941 is filed quarterly with the following deadlines:

Quarter Period Covered Due Date
Q1 January - March April 30
Q2 April - June July 31
Q3 July - September October 31
Q4 October - December January 31

If a due date falls on a weekend or federal holiday, the deadline moves to the next business day.

Tax Deposit Requirements

While you file Form 941 quarterly, you must deposit employment taxes more frequently:

Monthly Depositor Schedule

If your total tax liability for the lookback period (July 1 through June 30 of the prior year) was $50,000 or less, you're a monthly depositor. Deposit taxes accumulated during a calendar month by the 15th of the following month.

Semi-Weekly Depositor Schedule

If your lookback period liability exceeded $50,000, you're a semi-weekly depositor:

  • Wages paid Wednesday through Friday: deposit by the following Wednesday
  • Wages paid Saturday through Tuesday: deposit by the following Friday

Next-Day Deposit Rule

If you accumulate $100,000 or more in taxes on any day, you must deposit by the next business day. This also automatically makes you a semi-weekly depositor for the rest of the year and the following year.

The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty (TFRP)

This is where 941 taxes become personally dangerous for business owners and managers. The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty makes individuals personally liable for unpaid trust fund taxes.

What Are Trust Fund Taxes?

Trust fund taxes are the amounts withheld from employee wages - the federal income tax withholding and the employee's share of Social Security and Medicare taxes. These are called "trust fund" taxes because the employer holds them in trust for the government until deposited.

Who Is a Responsible Person?

The IRS can assess the TFRP against any "responsible person" who willfully fails to collect, account for, and pay over trust fund taxes. This includes:

  • Business owners
  • Corporate officers (CEO, CFO, etc.)
  • Directors with financial authority
  • Partners in a partnership
  • Employees with check-signing authority or financial decision-making power
  • Anyone who could have paid the taxes but chose to pay other creditors instead

TFRP Amount

The penalty equals 100% of the trust fund taxes not paid. If a company owes $50,000 in 941 taxes and $25,000 is the trust fund portion, responsible individuals can be personally assessed $25,000 each (yes, the IRS can pursue multiple people for the same liability).

Penalties for Non-Compliance

The IRS imposes significant penalties for 941 tax problems:

Failure to Deposit Penalty

  • 1-5 days late: 2% of unpaid taxes
  • 6-15 days late: 5% of unpaid taxes
  • More than 15 days late: 10% of unpaid taxes
  • More than 10 days after first IRS notice: 15% of unpaid taxes

Failure to File Penalty

5% of unpaid tax per month, up to 25% maximum.

Interest

Interest accrues daily on unpaid taxes and compounds quarterly.

Resolving 941 Tax Debt

If your business has fallen behind on 941 taxes, options exist but require immediate action:

  • Full Payment: The IRS strongly prefers full payment of payroll tax debt.
  • Installment Agreement: Payment plans are possible but require current compliance going forward.
  • Offer in Compromise: Settling for less is possible but more difficult for payroll taxes than other tax types.
  • Currently Not Collectible: Temporary suspension of collection if the business truly cannot pay.

Critical: The IRS requires businesses to become current on all payroll tax deposits before approving any payment arrangement. You cannot set up a payment plan while continuing to fall behind.

How to Complete Form 941

Key sections of the form include:

  • Part 1: Report total wages, tips, and compensation; federal income tax withheld; taxable Social Security and Medicare wages; and calculate total taxes.
  • Part 2: Deposit schedule and tax liability by month.
  • Part 3: Business information and seasonal employer status.
  • Part 4: Third-party designee authorization.
  • Part 5: Signature of business owner or authorized representative.

Key Points to Remember

Personal Liability Risk

The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty can hold you personally responsible for unpaid 941 taxes, even if your business closes.

Deposits Due Before Returns

Tax deposits are due monthly or semi-weekly, not quarterly. Filing the return doesn't mean you can wait to pay.

Quarterly Filing Schedule

Form 941 is due by the end of the month following each quarter: April 30, July 31, October 31, January 31.

Current Compliance Required

To resolve past-due 941 debt, you must first get current on all ongoing payroll tax deposits.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is IRS Form 941?

IRS Form 941 is the Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return, used to report income taxes withheld from employees' wages, as well as both the employer and employee portions of Social Security and Medicare taxes. Most employers with employees must file Form 941 quarterly to report these payroll taxes to the IRS.

When is Form 941 due?

Form 941 is due quarterly: April 30 (Q1 - January through March), July 31 (Q2 - April through June), October 31 (Q3 - July through September), and January 31 (Q4 - October through December). If the due date falls on a weekend or holiday, the deadline moves to the next business day.

What is the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty?

The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty (TFRP) holds responsible individuals personally liable for unpaid trust fund taxes - the income tax and employee share of Social Security and Medicare withheld from wages. The IRS can collect this 100% penalty from business owners, officers, and anyone with authority over financial decisions who willfully failed to pay these taxes.

How often must I deposit 941 taxes?

Deposit frequency depends on your total tax liability during a lookback period. Most employers are either monthly depositors (deposit by the 15th of the following month) or semi-weekly depositors (deposit within days of payroll based on payday). New employers typically start as monthly depositors. Very small employers with less than $2,500 quarterly liability may pay with the return.

What happens if I don't pay 941 taxes?

Unpaid 941 taxes result in severe consequences: failure-to-deposit penalties up to 15%, failure-to-file penalties up to 25%, daily interest charges, personal liability through the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty, and aggressive IRS collection actions. The IRS considers payroll tax non-compliance extremely serious because these taxes include money withheld from employees.
