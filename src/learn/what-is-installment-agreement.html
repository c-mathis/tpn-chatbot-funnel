When you owe taxes but cannot afford to pay the full amount immediately, an Installment Agreement offers a structured way to become current with the IRS. This payment plan divides your tax debt into monthly payments spread over an extended period, making it more manageable for your budget.

How Does an IRS Installment Agreement Work?

An Installment Agreement is a formal arrangement between you and the IRS that allows you to pay your tax debt in monthly installments. Once approved, you make consistent payments until your balance is paid in full, including any accrued interest and penalties.

The IRS offers several types of installment agreements to accommodate different financial situations:

Guaranteed Installment Agreement

If you owe $10,000 or less in combined tax, penalties, and interest, and can pay it off within 3 years, the IRS must grant you an installment agreement. This is a right, not a privilege, as long as you've filed all required returns and haven't had an installment agreement in the past 5 years.

Streamlined Installment Agreement

For taxpayers owing $50,000 or less, streamlined agreements offer a simplified application process. You won't need to provide detailed financial statements, and approval is generally quick. Your monthly payment is typically calculated to pay off the debt within 72 months (6 years) or before the collection statute expires, whichever is shorter.

Non-Streamlined Installment Agreement

If you owe more than $50,000, or need lower payments than the streamlined option allows, you'll need a non-streamlined agreement. This requires submitting Form 433-F (Collection Information Statement) with detailed information about your income, expenses, and assets. The IRS will determine your payment amount based on what you can actually afford.

Partial Payment Installment Agreement (PPIA)

When your financial situation won't allow you to fully pay your tax debt before the 10-year collection period expires, you may qualify for a PPIA. This agreement allows payments based on your ability to pay, with the remaining balance potentially being forgiven when the statute expires.

Requirements to Qualify

To set up an installment agreement with the IRS, you must meet certain requirements:

  • Filed all tax returns: All required returns must be filed before the IRS will approve a payment plan
  • Not in bankruptcy: You cannot have an open bankruptcy case
  • Current year compliance: If self-employed, you must be making required estimated tax payments
  • Employer withholding: Your W-4 withholding must be correct to avoid future balances

How to Apply for an Installment Agreement

You can apply for an installment agreement in several ways:

  • Online: Use the IRS Online Payment Agreement tool for balances under $50,000
  • By phone: Call the number on your IRS notice
  • By mail: Submit Form 9465 (Installment Agreement Request)
  • With professional help: A tax professional can negotiate terms on your behalf

Costs and Fees

Setting up an installment agreement involves certain fees:

  • Online setup fee: $31 for direct debit, $130 for other payment methods
  • Phone/mail setup fee: $107 for direct debit, $178 for other methods
  • Low-income fee: Reduced to $43 (may be waived) for qualifying taxpayers
  • Restructuring fee: $10 for reinstating or revising an existing agreement

Interest (currently around 8% annually) and the failure-to-pay penalty (reduced to 0.25% per month while in an agreement) continue to accrue on your unpaid balance.

Benefits of an Installment Agreement

An installment agreement offers several advantages over ignoring your tax debt:

  • Stops aggressive IRS collection actions like levies and seizures
  • Provides predictable monthly payments you can budget for
  • Prevents new tax liens if you choose direct debit and owe under $25,000
  • Demonstrates good faith to the IRS
  • Reduces the failure-to-pay penalty rate by half

Key Points to Remember

Multiple Options

The IRS offers several installment agreement types including guaranteed, streamlined, and non-streamlined to fit different financial situations.

$50K Threshold

Owing $50,000 or less qualifies you for streamlined processing with no detailed financial disclosure required.

Interest Accrues

Interest and penalties continue to accrue while on a payment plan, but the failure-to-pay penalty is reduced by half.

Stops Collections

Once approved, an installment agreement prevents aggressive IRS collection actions like levies and bank seizures.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a streamlined and non-streamlined installment agreement?

A streamlined installment agreement is available for taxpayers who owe $50,000 or less and can be set up quickly without detailed financial documentation. Non-streamlined agreements are for larger debts or situations requiring negotiation, and require submitting Form 433-F with detailed financial information for IRS review.

How much will my monthly payment be on an IRS installment agreement?

For streamlined agreements, your monthly payment is typically calculated by dividing your total tax debt by 72 months (6 years). For non-streamlined agreements, the IRS analyzes your income and expenses to determine what you can afford. Payments must be enough to pay off your debt before the 10-year collection statute expires.

Will interest and penalties continue while I'm on a payment plan?

Yes, interest and the failure-to-pay penalty continue to accrue on any unpaid balance while you're on an installment agreement. However, the failure-to-pay penalty rate is reduced by half (from 0.5% to 0.25% per month) while you're in compliance with your agreement.

Can the IRS reject my installment agreement request?

Yes, the IRS can reject your request if you haven't filed all required tax returns, if you're in bankruptcy, or if your proposed payment amount is too low. They may also reject if you defaulted on a previous agreement. However, you have the right to appeal a rejection within 30 days.

What happens if I miss a payment on my installment agreement?

Missing a payment can put your agreement in default status. The IRS typically sends a notice giving you 30 days to catch up before terminating the agreement. If terminated, they can resume collection actions including levies and garnishments. Contact the IRS immediately if you can't make a payment to discuss options.
